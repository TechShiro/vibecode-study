RSA and ElGamal Digital Signature Schemes

The two founding schemes, each worked end to end with numbers — key generation, signing, verification, and the algebra that proves the verification works. Plus the nonce-reuse attack that recovers ElGamal’s private key, worked in full.

Exam strategy

Both schemes are guaranteed numericals. RSA signing is the easier of the two and is often a 5-mark sub-question; ElGamal is a full 10-mark question because it has more moving parts. Always write the formula, then substitute, then check the verification condition — a verification that comes out equal is your own proof that the arithmetic is right. Learn the ElGamal verification equation αM ≡ yrrs as a unit; it is the line most often forgotten.

Part 1 — The RSA signature scheme

The scheme Key generation: choose primes p, q; n = pq; φ(n) = (p−1)(q−1);
choose e with gcd(e, φ(n)) = 1; find d with ed ≡ 1 mod φ(n).
Public key (e, n) · Private key (d, n)

Sign:   S = [H(M)]d mod n   —  private key, so only the owner can do it
Verify:   compute Se mod n and compare with H(M′). Equal → valid.

Note the exact reversal of RSA encryption: encryption raises to e (public) and decryption to d (private); signing raises to d and verification to e. Same algorithm, opposite key order, opposite purpose.

Numerical 1 — RSA signature, worked in full

Q. Take p = 17 and q = 11. Using e = 7, generate the key pair and sign the message digest M = 88. Verify the signature.

Step 1 — the modulus. n = 17 × 11 = 187

Step 2 — the totient. φ(n) = 16 × 10 = 160

Step 3 — check e. gcd(7, 160) = 1 ✓, so e = 7 is valid. Public key = (7, 187)

Step 4 — find d such that 7d ≡ 1 mod 160. Try multiples of 160 plus 1 and divide by 7: 161 ÷ 7 = 23 exactly. So d = 23, and 7 × 23 = 161 = 160 + 1 ✓. Private key = (23, 187)

Step 5 — sign: S = 8823 mod 187, by square-and-multiply. Write 23 = 16 + 4 + 2 + 1.

Sanity check: verification must return the original digest. If it returns anything else, recheck d — the commonest error is a d that does not satisfy ed ≡ 1 mod φ(n).

Part 2 — The ElGamal signature scheme

Published by Taher ElGamal in 1985, based on the discrete logarithm problem rather than factoring. It is the ancestor of Schnorr, DSA and ECDSA, so understanding it makes the next two lessons almost free.

The scheme Key generation: choose a large prime p and a primitive root α of p.
Choose a private key x with 1 < x < p−1, and compute y = αx mod p.
Public key (p, α, y) · Private key x

Sign M: choose a random k with gcd(k, p−1) = 1, then
r = αk mod p    s = k−1(M − x·r) mod (p−1)
The signature is the pair (r, s).

Verify: accept if   αM ≡ yr · rs (mod p)
The two moduli — the trap in every ElGamal question

r is computed mod p; s is computed mod (p−1). The exponent arithmetic lives mod p−1 (by Fermat’s little theorem) while the group arithmetic lives mod p. Mixing them up is the single most common way to lose this numerical. The verification, in contrast, is entirely mod p.

Numerical 2 — ElGamal signature, worked in full

Q. Take p = 19 with primitive root α = 2. Alice’s private key is x = 5. She signs the message digest M = 7 using k = 5. Find the signature and verify it.

Step 1 — the public key. y = αx mod p = 25 = 32 ≡ 13 mod 19. Public key = (19, 2, 13).

Step 2 — check k. p − 1 = 18, and gcd(5, 18) = 1 ✓, so k = 5 is usable.

Step 3 — compute r.   r = αk mod p = 25 = 32 ≡ 13

Step 4 — find k−1 mod 18. Try multiples: 5 × 11 = 55 = 54 + 1 ≡ 1 mod 18, so k−1 = 11.

Step 5 — compute s.
s = k−1(M − x·r) mod 18 = 11 × (7 − 5 × 13) mod 18 = 11 × (7 − 65) = 11 × (−58) mod 18
−58 mod 18: −58 + 72 = 14.   So s = 11 × 14 = 154 mod 18 = 154 − 144 = 10

Signature = (r, s) = (13, 10). Alice sends {M = 7, (13, 10)}.

Step 6 — verify: is αM ≡ yrrs mod 19?

Left side: αM = 27 = 128 ≡ 128 − 114 = 14

Right side: yrrs = 1313 × 1310 mod 19. Build the powers of 13:
132 = 169 ≡ 17;   134 = 172 = 289 ≡ 4;   138 = 42 = 16
1310 = 138 · 132 = 16 × 17 = 272 ≡ 6
1313 = 138 · 134 · 13 = 16 × 4 × 13 = 832 ≡ 832 − 817 = 15
Product: 15 × 6 = 90 ≡ 90 − 76 = 14

14 = 14, so the signature is valid. ✓

Why the verification works

The derivation, worth two lines in a long answer From s = k−1(M − xr) mod (p−1), multiply by k:   ks ≡ M − xr, so M ≡ xr + ks (mod p−1).
Then   αM = αxr + ks = (αx)r(αk)s = yrrs (mod p) — which is the verification equation.

The nonce k — and the attack when it repeats

Three rules for k, each worth a mark

(i) k must be random, (ii) k must be kept secret, and (iii) k must never be reused for a second message. Breaking any one of them reveals the private key x — a total break, the worst forgery level of Lesson 11.

Numerical 3 — recovering the private key from a reused k

Q. Alice signs two digests M1 = 7 and M2 = 8 with the same k = 5, giving (13, 10) and (13, 3). Recover k and her private key x.

Step 1 — spot the reuse. Both signatures share the same r = 13. Since r = αk, an identical r means an identical k. That is the giveaway an attacker looks for.

Step 2 — subtract the two s equations. With s1 = k−1(M1 − xr) and s2 = k−1(M2 − xr), the unknown xr cancels:
s1 − s2 ≡ k−1(M1 − M2)  ⇒  k = (M1 − M2)(s1 − s2)−1 mod (p−1)

s1 − s2 = 10 − 3 = 7.   7−1 mod 18 = 13 (7 × 13 = 91 = 90 + 1 ✓).
k = (7 − 8) × 13 = −13 ≡ −13 + 18 = 5 — the nonce is recovered.

Step 3 — recover x from either signature:   x = (M1 − k·s1) · r−1 mod (p−1)
M1 − ks1 = 7 − 50 = −43 ≡ −43 + 54 = 11.   r−1 = 13−1 mod 18 = 7 (13 × 7 = 91 ✓).
x = 11 × 7 = 77 ≡ 77 − 72 = 5

x = 5 — Alice’s private key, recovered from two signatures and a page of arithmetic. This is exactly how the PlayStation 3 code-signing key was extracted in 2010, and why RFC 6979 now specifies deterministic nonces derived from the message and the key.

Check yourself

1. With p = 17, q = 11 and e = 7, the RSA private exponent d is:

d is the inverse of e modulo φ(n) = 160, not modulo n = 187. That substitution is the classic slip in RSA questions.

2. In the ElGamal signature scheme, s is computed modulo:

Exponents live mod p − 1 by Fermat's little theorem; group elements live mod p. Option three describes DSA, which introduces a separate q.

3. The ElGamal verification condition is:

It follows from M ≡ xr + ks mod (p − 1): raise α to both sides and the right-hand side becomes y^r r^s. The check itself is entirely mod p.

4. Two ElGamal signatures from the same signer share the same value of r. This tells an attacker that:

r = α^k, so equal r means equal k. Subtracting the two s equations cancels xr, giving k and then x — a total break, as the worked numerical shows.

5. An RSA signature is deterministic, which means:

There is no random input to RSA signing. ElGamal, Schnorr, DSA and ECDSA all inject a fresh k, so their signatures differ every time.

6. The security of the ElGamal signature scheme rests on the difficulty of:

ElGamal is a discrete-log scheme; RSA is the factoring one. Collisions in the hash are a separate weakness that affects every signature scheme equally.

Recall without looking

State RSA key generation, signing and verification.

Choose primes p and q; n = pq; φ(n) = (p−1)(q−1); choose e coprime to φ(n); find d with ed ≡ 1 mod φ(n). Public key (e, n), private key (d, n). Sign: S = [H(M)]ᵈ mod n. Verify: compute Sᵉ mod n and compare with the digest of the received message.

Work the RSA signature for p = 17, q = 11, e = 7, M = 88.

n = 187, φ(n) = 160, d = 23 since 7 × 23 = 161 ≡ 1 mod 160. S = 88²³ mod 187 = 11, by square-and-multiply through 88² = 77, 88⁴ = 132, 88⁸ = 33, 88¹⁶ = 154. Verification: 11⁷ mod 187 = 88, which matches the digest, so the signature is valid.

Prove that RSA verification works.

Sᵉ = (Mᵈ)ᵉ = M^{de}. Since ed ≡ 1 mod φ(n), write de = 1 + kφ(n), so M^{de} = M·(M^{φ(n)})ᵏ ≡ M·1ᵏ = M mod n by Euler’s theorem.

State the ElGamal signature scheme in full.

Public: prime p, primitive root α, and y = αˣ mod p; private: x. To sign digest M, pick random k with gcd(k, p−1) = 1, compute r = α^k mod p and s = k⁻¹(M − xr) mod (p−1); the signature is (r, s). Verify by testing α^M ≡ y^r·r^s mod p.

Work the ElGamal signature for p = 19, α = 2, x = 5, M = 7, k = 5.

y = 2⁵ = 13. gcd(5, 18) = 1 so k is valid. r = 2⁵ mod 19 = 13. k⁻¹ mod 18 = 11. s = 11(7 − 65) mod 18 = 11 × 14 = 154 ≡ 10. Signature (13, 10). Verify: α^M = 2⁷ ≡ 14, and y^r r^s = 13¹³ × 13¹⁰ = 15 × 6 = 90 ≡ 14. Equal, so valid.

Show how a reused k breaks ElGamal.

Two signatures with the same k share the same r. Subtracting gives s₁ − s₂ ≡ k⁻¹(M₁ − M₂), so k = (M₁ − M₂)(s₁ − s₂)⁻¹ mod (p−1); then x = (M₁ − k s₁)r⁻¹ mod (p−1). With p = 19, M₁ = 7, M₂ = 8, s₁ = 10, s₂ = 3 and r = 13: k = (−1)(7)⁻¹ = −13 ≡ 5, and x = 11 × 7 = 77 ≡ 5. The private key is fully recovered.

Give six differences between RSA and ElGamal signatures.

Factoring versus discrete logarithm; one value versus a pair (r, s); deterministic versus randomised with a fresh k; identical versus different signatures on a repeated message; verification recovering the digest versus checking a congruence; and RSA doubling as an encryption scheme where ElGamal signing does not.
