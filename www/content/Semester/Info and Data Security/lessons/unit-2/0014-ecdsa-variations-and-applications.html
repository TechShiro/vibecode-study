Exam strategy

Part 1 — ECDSA

ECDSA is DSA with the group changed. Every modular exponentiation gk mod p becomes a scalar multiplication kG on an elliptic curve, and the modulus q becomes n, the order of the base point. Nothing else moves. It is standardised in FIPS 186 and ANSI X9.62, and is what secures TLS certificates, Bitcoin and Ethereum.

The scheme Domain parameters: curve Ep(a, b), base point G of prime order n.
Keys: private d (1 < d < n); public Q = dG.

Numerical — ECDSA, worked in full

Q. On the curve E17(2, 2): y2 = x3 + 2x + 2 mod 17, with G = (5, 1) of order n = 19, Alice’s private key is d = 7. She signs a message with digest H(M) = 5 using k = 3. Find the signature and verify it.

Step 0 — the multiples of G we will need (from Lesson 8’s double-and-add):

k1234781318
kG(5,1)(6,3)(10,6)(3,1)(0,6)(13,7)(16,4)(5,16)

Step 1 — the public key. Q = dG = 7G = (0, 6)

Step 2 — compute R and r. R = kG = 3G = (10, 6), so r = xR mod n = 10 mod 19 = 10  (r ≠ 0 ✓)

Step 3 — compute k−1 mod n. 3 × 13 = 39 = 38 + 1 ≡ 1 mod 19, so k−1 = 13

Step 4 — compute s.
s = k−1[H(M) + d·r] mod n = 13 × [5 + 7 × 10] mod 19 = 13 × 75 mod 19
75 mod 19 = 75 − 57 = 18;   s = 13 × 18 = 234 mod 19 = 234 − 228 = 6

Signature = (r, s) = (10, 6).

Step 5 — verification. w = s−1 mod n. 6 × 16 = 96 = 95 + 1 ≡ 1 mod 19, so w = 16

Step 6 — u1 and u2.
u1 = H(M)·w mod n = 5 × 16 = 80 mod 19 = 4  (80 − 76)
u2 = r·w mod n = 10 × 16 = 160 mod 19 = 8  (160 − 152)

Step 7 — compute X = u1G + u2Q.
u1G = 4G = (3, 1)
u2Q = 8 × (0,6) = 8 × 7G = 56G; and 56 mod 19 = 56 − 38 = 18, so u2Q = 18G = (5, 16)

Add them: (3, 1) + (5, 16).
λ = (16 − 1)(5 − 3)−1 = 15 × 2−1 ≡ 15 × 9 = 135 ≡ 16 mod 17  (2−1 = 9; 135 − 119 = 16)
x3 = λ2 − x1 − x2 = 256 − 3 − 5 = 248 ≡ 10  (248 − 238)
y3 = λ(x1 − x3) − y1 = 16(3 − 10) − 1 = −113 ≡ 6  (−113 + 119)
X = (10, 6)

Step 8 — compare. xX mod n = 10 mod 19 = 10 = r. Signature valid. ✓

Sanity checks: X should come out equal to R = kG itself — here (10,6) both times, which is the fastest way to spot an error. And both r and s must be non-zero mod n; if either is zero, choose a new k.

Why it works, and the two warnings

The derivation From s = k−1(H + dr) mod n:   k ≡ s−1H + s−1dr = u1 + du2 (mod n)
so   u1G + u2Q = u1G + u2(dG) = (u1 + du2)G = kG = R.
  • k must be random, secret and never repeated. Two signatures sharing an r reveal k, and then d = (sk − H)r−1 mod n — a total break. This exact failure exposed Sony’s PlayStation 3 signing key in 2010 and has drained Bitcoin wallets built on weak random number generators. RFC 6979 answers it with deterministic nonces derived from the key and the message.
  • Signature malleability: if (r, s) verifies, so does (r, n − s). Bitcoin therefore requires the “low-s” form. Worth a line if a question asks about ECDSA weaknesses.

Part 3 — Variations of digital signatures

VariationWhat it doesWhere it is used
Blind signature (Chaum, 1983)The signer signs a blinded message without seeing its content; the requester unblinds afterwards to get a valid signature on the originalE-cash, e-voting — the bank certifies a coin without linking it to the spender. (Technically the same algebra as the blinding attack of Lesson 11, used deliberately.)
Proxy signatureA signer delegates signing authority to a proxy, who signs on their behalf; verifiers can tell it was the proxyA manager on leave delegating approvals; distributed and mobile agent systems
Group signatureAny member of a group can sign anonymously on the group’s behalf; only the group manager can open the signature and identify the signerAnonymous attestation, whistleblowing with accountability, vehicle-to-vehicle networks
Ring signatureLike a group signature but with no manager and no way to revoke anonymity — the verifier learns only that someone in the ring signedMonero transactions; anonymous leaks
Undeniable signatureVerification requires the signer’s cooperation, but a disavowal protocol stops them denying a genuine signatureLicensed software: only the buyer can confirm authenticity
Multi-signatureSeveral signers sign one document; all signatures are requiredJoint bank accounts, multi-party contracts, Bitcoin multisig wallets
Threshold signatureAny t of n key-share holders can jointly produce one signature; fewer than t cannotCustody of high-value keys, distributed CAs
Aggregate signatureMany signatures on many messages are compressed into one short signatureBlockchain block compression (BLS signatures)
One-time signature (Lamport)A key pair usable for exactly one signature; built from hash functions onlyPost-quantum schemes and hash-based signatures (XMSS, SPHINCS+)

Part 4 — Applications

DomainHow digital signatures are used
Web security — SSL/TLSA CA signs the server’s certificate; the browser verifies it, then the handshake is signed with ECDSA or RSA. The whole PKI is a chain of signatures.
Software and code signingPublishers sign executables, drivers, app-store packages and firmware; secure boot refuses unsigned code. This is where the Sony PS3 key loss mattered.
Email securityPGP and S/MIME sign messages for authenticity and non-repudiation — the subject of Unit IV.
E-governance in IndiaThe IT Act, 2000 gives digital signatures legal standing. Digital Signature Certificates (DSC) from licensed CAs are mandatory for income-tax e-filing, GST returns, MCA/ROC filings, EPFO and e-tendering; Aadhaar eSign provides an online alternative.
Documents and contractsSigned PDFs, e-agreements and digitally signed university mark sheets; the signature both authenticates and detects any later alteration.
Blockchain and cryptocurrencyEvery Bitcoin or Ethereum transaction is signed with ECDSA over secp256k1; the signature is the proof of ownership of the coins.
Identity documentsE-passports, national ID smart cards and SIM applets carry signed data objects verified offline.
ProtocolsIPsec IKE, SSH host keys, DNSSEC and Kerberos-adjacent PKINIT all rest on signatures.

Check yourself

1. In ECDSA, the value r is obtained as:

R = kG, and r = x_R mod n. It mirrors DSA, where r = (g^k mod p) mod q — a group element crushed down into the small modulus.

2. On E₁₇(2,2) with G = (5,1), n = 19, d = 7, k = 3 and H(M) = 5, the signature is:

3G = (10,6), so r = 10; k⁻¹ = 13 mod 19, and s = 13(5 + 70) = 13 × 18 ≡ 6. The signature is (r, s) = (10, 6) — in that order.

3. ECDSA verification succeeds when the computed point X = u₁G + u₂Q satisfies:

u₁ + du₂ ≡ k mod n, so u₁G + u₂Q = kG = R. Comparing x-coordinates mod n is exactly the DSA test v = r.

4. A blind signature allows:

The other three describe group, undeniable and aggregate signatures. Blind signatures underpin e-cash and e-voting, and use the same algebra as the blinding attack — deliberately.

5. The difference between a group signature and a ring signature is that:

Both hide the individual signer, but only the group scheme keeps a trapdoor for accountability. Monero uses ring signatures precisely because nobody can open them.

6. Reusing k across two ECDSA signatures is catastrophic because:

Equal k gives equal R and so equal r; subtracting the s equations yields k, and then d = (sk − H)r⁻¹ mod n. This is how the PS3 signing key was recovered.

Recall without looking

State the ECDSA signing and verification algorithms.

Domain: curve E_p(a,b), base point G of order n. Private d, public Q = dG. Sign with a fresh k: R = kG, r = x_R mod n, s = k⁻¹[H(M) + dr] mod n, signature (r, s). Verify: w = s⁻¹ mod n, u₁ = H(M)w mod n, u₂ = rw mod n, X = u₁G + u₂Q; accept if x_X mod n = r.

Work the ECDSA example on E₁₇(2,2), G = (5,1), n = 19, d = 7, k = 3, H(M) = 5.

Q = 7G = (0,6). R = 3G = (10,6) so r = 10. k⁻¹ = 13 mod 19, and s = 13(5 + 70) = 13 × 18 ≡ 6, giving (10, 6). Verify: w = 16, u₁ = 4, u₂ = 8; u₁G = 4G = (3,1) and u₂Q = 18G = (5,16); their sum is (10,6), whose x-coordinate 10 equals r.

Map DSA onto ECDSA.

g^k mod p becomes kG; y = gˣ mod p becomes Q = dG; the subgroup order q becomes n, the order of G; r = (g^k mod p) mod q becomes the x-coordinate of kG mod n; and the verification value v becomes the x-coordinate of u₁G + u₂Q. The structure of the equations is unchanged.

Give the two ECDSA-specific warnings.

The nonce k must be random, secret and never reused — two signatures sharing r give k and then d = (sk − H)r⁻¹ mod n, a total break, as happened to Sony’s PS3 key; RFC 6979 deterministic nonces are the standard fix. And signatures are malleable: if (r, s) verifies then so does (r, n − s), which is why Bitcoin mandates the low-s form.

Name six variations of digital signatures with one use each.

Blind signature — e-cash and e-voting; proxy signature — delegated approval; group signature — anonymous signing with a manager who can identify the signer; ring signature — anonymity with no manager, as in Monero; undeniable signature — verification needing the signer’s cooperation, used for licensed software; multi-signature and threshold signature — joint accounts and distributed key custody; aggregate signature — compressing many signatures into one.

List six applications of digital signatures.

SSL/TLS certificates and the PKI chain; code, driver and firmware signing including secure boot; email security through PGP and S/MIME; Indian e-governance under the IT Act 2000 — DSCs for income-tax e-filing, GST, MCA filings and e-tendering, plus Aadhaar eSign; signed PDFs, contracts and certificates; and blockchain transactions signed with ECDSA.

Compare the five signature schemes.

RSA rests on factoring, gives one large value, is deterministic and can also encrypt. ElGamal rests on the discrete log, gives a pair the size of p, and needs a fresh k. Schnorr and DSA work in a subgroup of order q, giving short pairs; Schnorr allows full precomputation and is provably secure in the random oracle model, while DSA became the FIPS standard. ECDSA is DSA on an elliptic curve, giving the same short signature at a far smaller key size, and is what TLS and Bitcoin use.
