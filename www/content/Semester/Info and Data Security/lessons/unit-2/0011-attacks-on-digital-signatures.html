Attacks on Digital Signatures

What the adversary is allowed to do, the four levels of success, and the specific attacks — existential forgery, the multiplicative property, blinding, birthday and replay — three of them worked with real numbers.

Exam strategy

Asked as “Explain the attacks on digital signatures”, usually 5–10 marks. Answer in two lists, not one: first the attack types (what the attacker can obtain), then the forgery levels (what counts as a break). Then give two or three concrete attacks with their countermeasures. That structure is what separates a full-mark answer from a list of half-remembered names.

Visual Taxonomy: Adversary Capabilities and Forgery Severity
• Total Break (Catastrophic) Adversary recovers signer's private key $PR_A$ directly. Can forge valid signatures on arbitrary messages indefinitely.
• Selective Forgery Adversary creates a valid signature for a specific target message chosen before attack, without knowing private key.
• Existential Forgery Adversary generates at least one valid message-signature pair $(M, S)$, but has zero control over message contents.

Part 1 — Attack types: what the attacker is given

These are ordered from weakest attacker to strongest. The scheme is judged by the strongest attack it survives.

Attack typeWhat the attacker hasComment
1. Key-only attackOnly the signer’s public key, which is published anywayThe weakest attacker. Any scheme broken here is worthless.
2. Known-message attackThe public key plus a set of messages and their valid signatures, but the attacker did not choose themRealistic — signed messages are often public
3. Generic chosen-message attackThe attacker chooses a list of messages in advance and obtains signatures on all of them — chosen before seeing the signer’s public key, so it is not aimed at a particular victimAlso called a directed attack when the list is chosen after seeing that user’s public key
4. Adaptive chosen-message attackThe attacker uses the signer as an oracle, choosing each new message after seeing the signatures on earlier onesThe strongest attack. A scheme secure against this is the modern standard

Part 2 — Forgery levels: what counts as success

LevelAttacker achievesSeverity
1. Total breakRecovers the private key itselfCatastrophic — the attacker becomes the signer permanently
2. Universal forgeryFinds an algorithm equivalent to signing — can sign any message without holding the keyNearly as bad as a total break
3. Selective forgeryForges a signature on a message of the attacker’s choosingSerious — this is what forging a cheque or contract means
4. Existential forgeryForges a signature on at least one message, possibly meaningless, that the attacker does not controlThe weakest break — but still a break, because the forged “message” may later prove useful
The sentence that ties the two lists together

A scheme is called secure if it resists existential forgery even under an adaptive chosen-message attack — the weakest kind of success against the strongest kind of attacker. Writing that one line shows the examiner you understand why there are two lists rather than one.

Part 3 — The specific attacks

Attack 1: existential forgery on textbook RSA

Raw RSA signing — signing the message directly instead of its hash — is existentially forgeable in one step: pick the signature first and derive the message from it.

Numerical 1 — existential forgery

Setup: p = 17, q = 11, so n = 187 and φ(n) = 160. Public key e = 7, private key d = 23 (7 × 23 = 161 = 160 + 1 ✓).

The attack: Eve does not know d. She simply picks any value as the signature, say S = 9, and computes the message it happens to sign using only the public key:

M = Se mod n = 97 mod 187 = 70

She publishes the pair (M = 70, S = 9). Anyone verifying computes 97 mod 187 = 70 = M, so the signature is valid — and Alice never signed anything.

Check: Alice’s genuine signature on 70 would be 7023 mod 187 = 9. The same pair. The forgery is indistinguishable from the real thing.

Why it is only existential: Eve cannot choose M — it fell out of her choice of S, and is almost certainly meaningless. The countermeasure is to hash first: she would need a message whose digest equals 70, which is a preimage attack on the hash.

Attack 2: the multiplicative property of RSA

The algebraic weakness sig(M1) × sig(M2) ≡ sig(M1 × M2) mod n
because (M1d)(M2d) = (M1M2)d — RSA is homomorphic under multiplication.
Numerical 2 — forging a signature from two known ones

Same keys: n = 187, e = 7, d = 23. Eve has seen Alice sign two harmless messages:

sig(3) = 323 mod 187 = 181   and   sig(5) = 523 mod 187 = 180

Eve multiplies the two signatures: 181 × 180 = 32580 ≡ 42 mod 187  (187 × 174 = 32538; 32580 − 32538 = 42).

She claims 42 is Alice’s signature on M = 3 × 5 = 15. Verify: 427 mod 187 = 15 ✓, and Alice’s genuine signature 1523 mod 187 is indeed 42.

Eve has forged a signature on a message Alice never saw, using only two signatures and a multiplication. Countermeasure: hash before signing — H(M1) × H(M2) is not the hash of anything in particular, so the algebra collapses. Modern RSA also adds structured padding (PSS).

Attack 3: the blinding attack (chosen-message)

Eve wants Alice’s signature on a message M that Alice would refuse to sign. She disguises it, gets the disguise signed, and removes the disguise.

Numerical 3 — blinding, worked

Same keys: n = 187, e = 7, d = 23. Target message M = 88.

Step 1 — blind it. Eve picks a random r = 2 and computes
M′ = re × M mod n = 27 × 88 = 128 × 88 = 11264 ≡ 44 mod 187

Step 2 — get it signed. M′ = 44 looks like random junk, so Alice signs it:
S′ = 4423 mod 187 = 22

Step 3 — unblind. Eve computes r−1 mod 187 = 94 (2 × 94 = 188 ≡ 1 ✓) and multiplies:
S = S′ × r−1 = 22 × 94 = 2068 ≡ 11 mod 187

Result: S = 11, which is exactly Alice’s genuine signature on 88 (8823 mod 187 = 11). Verify: 117 mod 187 = 88 ✓

Why it works: S′ = (reM)d = redMd = r · Md, so dividing by r leaves Md. Countermeasures: never sign random-looking data supplied by others, and always hash and pad the message first. (Note that the same trick, used deliberately, is what makes blind signatures possible in e-cash and e-voting — Lesson 14.)

Attack 4: the birthday attack on the hash

Signature security is capped by the hash. Eve prepares two versions of a contract — one harmless, one fraudulent — and varies spacing, synonyms or invisible characters until both have the same digest. This costs only 2n/2 work for an n-bit digest, by the birthday paradox of Unit I. Alice signs the harmless one; the signature verifies equally on the fraudulent one. Alice’s key was never broken — only her hash function. Countermeasure: a collision-resistant hash of at least 256 bits, and never signing a document supplied wholesale by the other party.

Attack 5: replay

The signature on a genuine message stays valid for ever, so a captured “transfer ₹10,000” message can be re-sent unchanged and will verify every time. Nothing about it is forged. Countermeasures: timestamps, nonces or sequence numbers inside the signed data, plus an expiry on the signature.

Attack 6: key compromise and repudiation

Two related problems from Lesson 10. A stolen private key allows unlimited backdated forgeries; a dishonest signer can falsely claim theft to repudiate a genuine signature. Countermeasures: trusted timestamping, immediate revocation through a CRL or OCSP, and a certificate authority that fixes validity periods.

Attack 7: nonce reuse in DSS-type schemes

Named here and worked in Lesson 13: in ElGamal, DSA and ECDSA the random k must be fresh and secret for every signature. Reuse across two messages lets an attacker solve two linear equations for k and then for the private key — a total break, the worst level of forgery. This is not theoretical: it is how the PlayStation 3 code-signing key was recovered in 2010.

Countermeasures, collected

2. Existential forgery means the attacker:

The other three are selective forgery, total break and universal forgery. Existential forgery is the weakest success — but still counts as breaking the scheme.

3. With n = 187, e = 7 and d = 23, if sig(3) = 181 and sig(5) = 180, then 181 × 180 mod 187 = 42 is a valid signature on:

(3^d)(5^d) = 15^d mod n. Verify: 42⁷ mod 187 = 15. Hashing before signing is what destroys this algebra.

4. In the blinding attack, Eve multiplies the target message by rᵉ before submitting it because:

(rᵉM)ᵈ = r^{ed}Mᵈ = r·Mᵈ, since ed ≡ 1 mod φ(n). Multiplying by r⁻¹ then strips the blind — as the worked example shows, recovering S = 11 for M = 88.

5. A birthday attack against a signature scheme succeeds by:

It attacks the hash, not the key: 2^(n/2) work by the birthday paradox. The victim signs one document and the signature is equally valid on the other.

6. A replayed signed message verifies correctly because:

Freshness is a separate property from authenticity. Timestamps, nonces or sequence numbers inside the signed data are what defeat replay.

Recall without looking

Name the four attack types on digital signatures.

Key-only, where the attacker has only the public key; known-message, where they also hold message–signature pairs they did not choose; chosen-message, generic or directed, where they choose the list of messages to be signed in advance; and adaptive chosen-message, where each new message is chosen after seeing the previous signatures. The last is the strongest.

Name the four levels of forgery.

Total break — the private key is recovered. Universal forgery — an algorithm equivalent to signing is found. Selective forgery — a signature is forged on a message the attacker chooses. Existential forgery — a signature is forged on at least one message, possibly meaningless. A scheme is secure if it resists existential forgery under an adaptive chosen-message attack.

Explain existential forgery against textbook RSA.

The attacker picks any value S as the signature and computes M = Sᵉ mod n using only the public key. The pair (M, S) verifies correctly although the owner never signed. With n = 187 and e = 7, choosing S = 9 gives M = 70. It is only existential because M cannot be chosen, and hashing before signing defeats it.

State RSA's multiplicative property and its consequence.

sig(M₁)·sig(M₂) ≡ sig(M₁M₂) mod n, because (M₁ᵈ)(M₂ᵈ) = (M₁M₂)ᵈ. An attacker who has seen signatures on two messages can forge one on their product — with n = 187, sig(3) = 181 and sig(5) = 180 give 181 × 180 ≡ 42 = sig(15). Hashing and PSS padding remove the algebraic structure.

Describe the blinding attack.

Eve blinds a target message M as M′ = rᵉM mod n for a random r, has the signer sign the innocuous-looking M′ to get S′ = r·Mᵈ, then unblinds with S = S′·r⁻¹ = Mᵈ. With n = 187, e = 7, M = 88 and r = 2: M′ = 44, S′ = 22, r⁻¹ = 94, and S = 11, the genuine signature. Never sign random-looking data supplied by others; hash and pad first.

Explain the birthday attack and the replay attack on signatures.

Birthday: the attacker produces two documents with the same digest — one harmless, one fraudulent — at a cost of only 2^(n/2), gets the harmless one signed, and reuses the signature on the other; countered by a 256-bit or larger collision-resistant hash. Replay: a genuine signed message is captured and re-sent, verifying every time because nothing is forged; countered by timestamps, nonces or sequence numbers within the signed data.

List the main countermeasures.

Hash before signing and use structured padding such as RSA-PSS; use a collision-resistant hash of at least 256 bits; include timestamps or nonces to stop replay; use certificates, trusted timestamping and revocation to handle key compromise and false repudiation; never reuse the random k; and use standardised schemes rather than hand-built ones.
