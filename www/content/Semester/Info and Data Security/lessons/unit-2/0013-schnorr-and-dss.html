Schnorr and the Digital Signature Standard

The two schemes that shrink ElGamal by working in a small subgroup of order q — Schnorr with its precomputation trick, DSA with its two-modulus structure — each worked end to end with numbers.

Exam strategy

DSS is the more likely of the two to appear as a full 10-mark numerical; Schnorr is usually 5 marks or a comparison. For DSA, memorise the five signing values (p, q, g, x, y) and the two-line signature (r, s), and above all the verification chain w → u1 → u2 → v. The examiner’s favourite one-liner is “why are r and s reduced mod q?” — because it makes the signature 320 bits instead of 2048.

The idea both schemes share

ElGamal works in the whole group Zp*, so both halves of its signature are the size of p — 2048 bits each today. Schnorr and DSA instead work in a small subgroup of prime order q inside that group. Exponent arithmetic then happens mod q, so the signature values are the size of q (160–256 bits), while the security still rests on the discrete logarithm in the large group mod p.

One sentence that answers half the questions in this lesson

p is large for security; q is small for efficiency. Everything exponent-sized is reduced mod q, everything group-sized mod p. That is why a DSA signature is 320 bits where an RSA signature of the same strength is 2048.

Part 1 — The Schnorr signature scheme

The scheme Global parameters: a prime p; a prime q dividing p − 1;
and α with αq ≡ 1 mod p, α ≠ 1 — a generator of the subgroup of order q.

Keys: private s (0 < s < q); public v = α−s mod p.

Sign M: pick a random r (0 < r < q), compute
x = αr mod p,   e = H(M ‖ x),   y = (r + s·e) mod q
The signature is the pair (e, y).

Verify: compute x′ = αyve mod p and accept if H(M ‖ x′) = e.
The two features that define Schnorr

(i) The public key is α−s, not αs — the negative exponent is what makes αyve collapse back to αr. (ii) The hash binds the message to the commitment x, so e = H(M ‖ x) — the message enters through the hash rather than through the arithmetic. Both are commonly missed.

Why verification works

The one-line proof αyve = αr + se · (α−s)e = αr + se − se = αr = x  (mod p)
so the verifier rebuilds x and the hash check must succeed.
Numerical 1 — Schnorr, worked in full

Q. Take p = 23, q = 11 and α = 4. Alice’s private key is s = 7. She signs a message with r = 3, and the hash gives e = H(M ‖ x) = 5. Find the signature and verify it.

Step 0 — check the parameters. q = 11 divides p − 1 = 22 ✓. And α = 4 has order 11: its powers are 4, 16, 18, 3, 12, 2, 8, 9, 13, 6, 1 — returning to 1 exactly at the eleventh, so αq ≡ 1 ✓.

(α is normally built as α = h(p−1)/q mod p; here h = 2 gives 22 = 4.)

Step 1 — the public key. v = α−s mod p = (47)−1 mod 23.
47: 42 = 16, 43 = 64 ≡ 18, 46 = 182 = 324 ≡ 2, so 47 = 2 × 4 = 8.
Now invert: 8 × 3 = 24 ≡ 1 mod 23, so v = 3.

Step 2 — the commitment. x = αr mod p = 43 = 64 ≡ 18

Step 3 — the hash. e = H(M ‖ x) = 5 (given).

Step 4 — the response. y = (r + s·e) mod q = (3 + 7 × 5) mod 11 = 38 mod 11 = 5

Signature = (e, y) = (5, 5).

Step 5 — verify. x′ = αyve mod p = 45 × 35 mod 23.
45 = 1024 ≡ 1024 − 1012 = 12  (23 × 44 = 1012)
35 = 243 ≡ 243 − 230 = 13
x′ = 12 × 13 = 156 ≡ 156 − 138 = 18

x′ = 18 = x, so H(M ‖ x′) = H(M ‖ x) = 5 = e. Signature valid. ✓

Why Schnorr is efficient — the precomputation argument

StageWorkComment
Before the message arrivesPick r, compute x = αr mod pThe only exponentiation, and it does not involve M — so it can be done in idle time and stored
When the message arrivesHash M ‖ x, then one multiplication and one addition mod qAlmost free — this is Schnorr’s selling point for smart cards
Signature size|e| + |q| — about 160 + 160 bitsShort, because both halves are subgroup-sized

Two more marks-worthy facts: Schnorr’s scheme is provably secure in the random oracle model (Unit I, Lesson 3), and it was patented until 2008 — which is precisely why NIST built DSA on ElGamal instead, and why Schnorr signatures only reached wide deployment recently, in Bitcoin’s Taproot upgrade.

Part 2 — DSS and the DSA algorithm

Terminology first, because examiners test it: DSS (Digital Signature Standard) is the FIPS 186 standard; DSA (Digital Signature Algorithm) is the algorithm inside it. DSA was designed by NIST/NSA in 1991, is based on ElGamal and Schnorr, and — unlike RSA — cannot be used for encryption or key exchange, only for signing.

DSA parameters and keys Global public parameters, shared by a group of users:
p — a prime, 1024 to 3072 bits  ·  q — a prime divisor of p − 1, 160 to 256 bits
g = h(p−1)/q mod p for some h, with g > 1 — so g has order q

Per user: private x (0 < x < q), public y = gx mod p
Per signature: a fresh random secret k (0 < k < q)
Signing and verification SIGN:   r = (gk mod p) mod q    s = k−1[H(M) + x·r] mod q
    Signature = (r, s)

VERIFY:   w = s−1 mod q
    u1 = [H(M) · w] mod q    u2 = (r · w) mod q
    v = [(gu1 · yu2) mod p] mod q
    Accept if v = r.
The two double reductions

Both r and v are computed mod p first, then mod q. Students routinely drop the second reduction and get v ≠ r on a perfectly good signature. Write the nested brackets exactly as above.

Numerical 2 — DSA, worked in full

Q. Take p = 23 and q = 11. With h = 2, find g. Alice’s private key is x = 2 and she signs a message whose digest is H(M) = 5, using k = 3. Find the signature and verify it.

Step 1 — check q | p − 1. p − 1 = 22 = 2 × 11 ✓

Step 2 — compute g. g = h(p−1)/q mod p = 222/11 = 22 = 4  (g > 1 ✓, and 4 has order 11 mod 23)

Step 3 — the public key. y = gx mod p = 42 = 16
Public: (p, q, g, y) = (23, 11, 4, 16); private: x = 2.

Step 4 — compute r.   r = (gk mod p) mod q = (43 mod 23) mod 11
43 = 64; 64 mod 23 = 64 − 46 = 18; then 18 mod 11 = 7.   r = 7

Step 5 — compute k−1 mod q. 3 × 4 = 12 ≡ 1 mod 11, so k−1 = 4

Step 6 — compute s.
s = k−1[H(M) + x·r] mod q = 4 × [5 + 2 × 7] mod 11 = 4 × 19 mod 11
19 mod 11 = 8, so s = 4 × 8 = 32 mod 11 = 10

Signature = (r, s) = (7, 10).

Step 7 — verification. Compute w = s−1 mod q. 10 × 10 = 100 = 99 + 1 ≡ 1 mod 11, so w = 10

Step 8 — u1 and u2.
u1 = H(M)·w mod q = 5 × 10 = 50 mod 11 = 6
u2 = r·w mod q = 7 × 10 = 70 mod 11 = 4

Step 9 — compute v.
gu1 = 46 mod 23: 43 ≡ 18, so 46 = 182 = 324 ≡ 324 − 322 = 2
yu2 = 164 mod 23: 162 = 256 ≡ 3, so 164 = 32 = 9
Product mod p: 2 × 9 = 18; then mod q: 18 mod 11 = 7

v = 7 = r, so the signature is valid. ✓

Sanity checks: both r and s must lie in 0 < value < q — if either comes out 0, the standard says discard k and start again. And v is compared with r, never with s.

Why DSA verification works

The derivation From s = k−1(H + xr) mod q we get k ≡ s−1(H + xr) = wH + wxr = u1 + xu2 (mod q).
Therefore   gu1yu2 = gu1gxu2 = gu1 + xu2 = gk (mod p)
Reducing both sides mod q gives v = r.

DSS features worth listing

  • Signature is only 2q bits — 320 bits with a 160-bit q, against 1024+ for RSA at comparable strength.
  • Signature only: DSA cannot encrypt or exchange keys. RSA can do all three.
  • Randomised: the same message signs differently every time, because k is fresh.
  • Signing is faster than verification — the reverse of RSA, and useful when a low-power device signs and a server verifies.
  • Global parameters are shared across a user community, so only y need be certified per user.
  • k must be random, secret and never reused — the same total break as ElGamal (Lesson 12): from two signatures sharing r, k = (H1 − H2)(s1 − s2)−1 mod q and then x = (sk − H)r−1 mod q.
  • Later editions of FIPS 186 admit RSA and ECDSA alongside DSA.

Check yourself

1. In the Schnorr scheme, the public key is:

The negative exponent is the whole trick: α^y v^e = α^{r+se}·α^{−se} = α^r = x, which the verifier then hashes.

2. Schnorr signing is fast once the message arrives because:

After the hash, signing costs one multiplication and one addition mod q — which is why Schnorr suits smart cards.

3. In DSA with p = 23, q = 11, g = 4 and k = 3, the value r is:

Reduce mod p first, then mod q. Dropping the second reduction (option two) or the first (option four) are the two standard errors.

4. DSA verification concludes by testing whether:

DSA never recovers the digest — that is the RSA approach. It rebuilds g^k in the subgroup and compares its reduction with r.

5. DSA signatures are short because:

Security comes from the size of p; compactness from working in the subgroup of order q. With a 160-bit q the signature is 320 bits.

6. Which statement about DSA is correct?

DSS is a signature standard only. It is randomised through k, and unlike RSA its signing is faster than its verification.

Recall without looking

State the Schnorr signature scheme.

Global: prime p, prime q dividing p − 1, and α with α^q ≡ 1 mod p. Private key s, public key v = α^(−s) mod p. To sign: pick random r, compute x = α^r mod p, e = H(M ‖ x), and y = (r + se) mod q; the signature is (e, y). Verify by computing x′ = α^y v^e mod p and checking H(M ‖ x′) = e.

Work the Schnorr example for p = 23, q = 11, α = 4, s = 7, r = 3, e = 5.

v = (4⁷)⁻¹ = 8⁻¹ = 3 mod 23. x = 4³ = 64 ≡ 18. y = (3 + 7×5) mod 11 = 38 mod 11 = 5, so the signature is (5, 5). Verify: α^y v^e = 4⁵ × 3⁵ = 12 × 13 = 156 ≡ 18 = x, so the hash check succeeds.

Why is Schnorr efficient, and what limited its adoption?

Its single exponentiation x = α^r is independent of the message and can be precomputed, leaving only a hash, one multiplication and one addition mod q at signing time; the signature is only about 2|q| bits. It is provably secure in the random oracle model, but was patented until 2008, which is why NIST based DSA on ElGamal instead.

Give the DSA parameters, keys and signing equations.

Global: prime p of 1024–3072 bits, prime q of 160–256 bits dividing p − 1, and g = h^((p−1)/q) mod p with g > 1. Private x with 0 < x < q, public y = gˣ mod p. To sign with a fresh random k: r = (g^k mod p) mod q and s = k⁻¹[H(M) + xr] mod q, giving the signature (r, s).

Give the DSA verification steps.

Compute w = s⁻¹ mod q, u₁ = H(M)·w mod q, u₂ = r·w mod q, and v = [(g^u1 · y^u2) mod p] mod q. Accept the signature if v = r. Note the double reduction — mod p and then mod q — in both r and v.

Work the DSA example for p = 23, q = 11, h = 2, x = 2, k = 3, H(M) = 5.

g = 2² = 4; y = 4² = 16. r = (4³ mod 23) mod 11 = 18 mod 11 = 7. k⁻¹ = 4 mod 11, so s = 4(5 + 14) mod 11 = 4 × 8 = 10. Signature (7, 10). Verify: w = 10, u₁ = 50 mod 11 = 6, u₂ = 70 mod 11 = 4, g^6 = 2 and y⁴ = 9 mod 23, product 18, and 18 mod 11 = 7 = r.

Prove DSA verification.

From s = k⁻¹(H + xr) mod q, k ≡ s⁻¹(H + xr) = wH + wxr = u₁ + xu₂ mod q. Hence g^{u₁}y^{u₂} = g^{u₁+xu₂} = g^k mod p, and reducing both sides mod q gives v = r.

Give six differences between RSA and DSA.

Factoring versus discrete logarithm; RSA does encryption, key exchange and signing while DSA signs only; 2048-bit versus 320-bit signatures; RSA signs slowly and verifies fast while DSA is the reverse; RSA is deterministic while DSA needs a fresh k; and RSA verification recovers the digest while DSA only tests v = r.
