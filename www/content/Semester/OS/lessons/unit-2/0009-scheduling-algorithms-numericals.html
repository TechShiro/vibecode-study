1. First Come First Served (FCFS)

Non-preemptive. The process that requests the CPU first gets it first. Implemented with a simple FIFO queue: a new PCB joins the tail, the CPU is given to the head. It is the simplest algorithm to write and the easiest to reason about — and usually the worst performer.

The convoy effect — name it in every FCFS answer

When one CPU-bound process holds the CPU, all the short I/O-bound processes queue behind it. The devices sit idle. When the long process finally releases the CPU and moves to I/O, the short processes rush through the CPU quickly and pile up on the devices instead, leaving the CPU idle. Lower CPU and device utilisation than if the short processes went first. This is the convoy effect, and it is FCFS's defining weakness.

2. Shortest Job First (SJF) — non-preemptive

Associate with each process the length of its next CPU burst; give the CPU to the process with the smallest. Ties are broken by FCFS. The proper name is shortest-next-CPU-burst scheduling, because it is the next burst that matters, not the total process length.

The property to quote

SJF is provably optimal — it gives the minimum average waiting time for a given set of processes. Moving a short process ahead of a long one always reduces the short process's waiting time by more than it increases the long one's. But it cannot be implemented for short-term scheduling, because there is no way to know the length of the next CPU burst. Both halves of that sentence carry marks.

Predicting the next burst — exponential averaging τn+1 = α tn + (1 − α) τn

tn = the actual length of the n-th CPU burst; τn = the predicted value for it; α, with 0 ≤ α ≤ 1, weights recent history.

α = 0 → τn+1 = τn: recent history has no effect.
α = 1 → τn+1 = tn: only the most recent burst counts.
α = 1/2 is the usual choice — recent and past history weighted equally.

3. Shortest Remaining Time First (SRTF) — preemptive SJF

Same rule, but re-evaluated every time a new process arrives. If the newcomer's burst is shorter than the remaining time of the running process, the running process is preempted. This is the hardest algorithm to trace by hand, so work it in a strict discipline: at every arrival instant, list every remaining time and pick the smallest.

4. Priority scheduling

A priority number is associated with each process, and the CPU goes to the highest-priority one. Convention: a smaller integer means a higher priority — but always state your convention, because some questions reverse it.

SJF is a special case of priority scheduling, in which the priority is the inverse of the predicted next CPU burst. Say that; it is a free mark.

Starvation and ageing — always mention both

The problem: indefinite blocking, or starvation. A low-priority process may wait for ever if high-priority processes keep arriving. The famous anecdote is worth a line: when MIT's IBM 7094 was shut down in 1973, a low-priority process submitted in 1967 was found still waiting.

The solution: ageing — gradually increase the priority of processes that have been waiting a long time. If priorities run 127 (low) to 0 (high), incrementing the priority of every waiting process by 1 every 15 minutes means even a priority-127 process reaches priority 0 within 32 hours.

5. Round Robin (RR)

FCFS with preemption. A small unit of time — the time quantum or time slice, typically 10 to 100 milliseconds — is defined. The ready queue is circular: the scheduler gives the CPU to the head process for at most one quantum. If the process finishes first, it releases the CPU voluntarily; otherwise the timer interrupts, the process is preempted and put at the tail of the ready queue.

The queue rule that decides the whole answer

When a process is preempted, any process that arrived during its quantum joins the ready queue before the preempted process does. Getting this order wrong is the single most common error in Round Robin numericals. Write the queue contents beside each step, and update it in that order: arrivals first, then the preempted process.

The waiting-time bound With n processes in the ready queue and a quantum of q, each process gets 1/n of the CPU time in chunks of at most q. No process waits more than (n − 1) × q time units for its next turn. That bound is why Round Robin gives good response time.

Recall without looking

Define the convoy effect and say which algorithm suffers it.

In FCFS, one long CPU-bound process holds the CPU while short I/O-bound processes queue behind it. The devices idle; then when the long process moves to I/O the short ones rush through and pile up on the devices, leaving the CPU idle. The result is lower utilisation of both.

State the two halves of the SJF result.

SJF is provably optimal — it gives the minimum average waiting time for a given set of processes. But it cannot be implemented for short-term scheduling, because there is no way to know the length of the next CPU burst; it is estimated by exponential averaging instead.

Write the exponential averaging formula and the meaning of its extremes.

τₙ₊₁ = αtₙ + (1 − α)τₙ. With α = 0 recent history has no effect and the prediction never changes; with α = 1 only the most recent burst counts; α = ½ weights recent and past history equally.

Define starvation and ageing.

Starvation, or indefinite blocking, is a low-priority process waiting for ever because higher-priority processes keep arriving. Ageing gradually increases the priority of processes that have waited a long time, guaranteeing that every process eventually runs.

Give the Round Robin queue rule and the waiting bound.

A preempted process joins the tail of the ready queue behind any process that arrived during its quantum. With n processes and quantum q, no process waits more than (n − 1) × q for its next turn.

What happens to Round Robin at each extreme of quantum size, and what is the rule of thumb?

A very large quantum makes it FCFS; a very small one approaches processor sharing but is dominated by context-switch overhead. The rule of thumb is that 80 % of CPU bursts should be shorter than the quantum.

Give the master comparison of the five algorithms.

FCFS: non-preemptive, selects on arrival, simplest and starvation-free, but suffers the convoy effect. SJF: non-preemptive, shortest next burst, optimal average waiting time, but unknowable and starves long jobs. SRTF: preemptive version, better still but more switches. Priority: either form, flexible but starves low-priority jobs unless aged. Round Robin: preemptive with a quantum, fair with bounded response time and no starvation, at the cost of turnaround time and context switches.
