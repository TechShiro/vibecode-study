Definition verbatim

Zadeh's definition — write it exactly “Soft computing is a collection of methodologies that aim to exploit the tolerance for imprecision, uncertainty and partial truth to achieve tractability, robustness and low solution cost.”

The guiding principle adds: … and better rapport with reality. Examiners notice that phrase.

Why it exists: conventional computing needs a precisely stated analytical model. Many real problems — recognising a face, driving a car, diagnosing a patient — have no such model, or one too costly to solve. Soft computing buys tractability by giving up exactness.

Characteristics of soft computing

  • Tolerant of imprecision, uncertainty, partial truth and approximation
  • Gives approximate rather than exact solutions
  • Biologically inspired — modelled on the human mind and on evolution
  • Learns from experience rather than being fully programmed
  • Fault tolerant and robust — degrades gracefully with noisy or missing data
  • Suited to parallel computation
  • Its constituents are complementary, not competitive, and are often combined

Hybrid computing combines the two — and combines the soft constituents with each other. Neuro-fuzzy systems (ANFIS, met in Unit II lesson 9) and genetic-fuzzy controllers are the standard examples.

The closing line for this answer

“Hard computing gives an exact answer to an exactly stated problem; soft computing gives a usable answer to a problem that cannot be stated exactly.”

The two lines that lift this answer

“The constituents are complementary, not competitive.”

“Fuzzy logic is knowledge representation without learning; a neural network is learning without knowledge representation.” — which is exactly why the two are combined into neuro-fuzzy systems.

The distinction examiners probe

Fuzzy vs rough. A fuzzy set handles a concept that is inherently vague: “tall” has no sharp boundary, so membership is graded. A rough set handles a concept that is crisp but whose boundary cannot be determined from the attributes available, so it is approximated from below (lower approximation — certainly in) and from above (upper approximation — possibly in). Vagueness in the concept versus insufficiency in the data.

The example to quote if only one is wanted: the Sendai subway in Japan (1987), whose fuzzy controller accelerated and braked more smoothly and used less power than a human driver — the demonstration that made fuzzy control commercially credible.
