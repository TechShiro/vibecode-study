Multi-Objective Genetic Algorithm (MOGA)

In classical single-objective optimization, the goal is to find one unique global optimum. However, real-world problems almost always require optimizing multiple conflicting objectives simultaneously (e.g., minimizing production cost while maximizing structural durability and fuel efficiency).

Concept of Pareto Dominance

When objectives conflict, no single solution is universally superior. Instead, we use the principle of Pareto Dominance introduced by Vilfredo Pareto:

Mathematical Definition of Dominance A solution u is said to dominate solution v (written as u ≺ v) if and only if:
1. u is no worse than v in all objective functions: fk(u) ≤ fk(v) ∀ k.
2. u is strictly better than v in at least one objective: ∃ j such that fj(u) < fj(v).
  • Non-Dominated Solution: A solution that is not dominated by any other feasible solution in the current population.
  • Pareto Optimal Set: The set of all non-dominated solutions across the entire feasible search space.
  • Pareto Front: The plot of the Pareto optimal set projected in the objective function space. Every point on the front represents an optimal trade-off.

NSGA-II (Non-dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II)

Developed by Kalyanmoy Deb et al. (2002), NSGA-II is the gold standard benchmark algorithm for multi-objective optimization due to its computational efficiency and preservation of diversity:

FeatureHow NSGA-II WorksWhy It Matters
Fast Non-Dominated Sorting Classifies the population into hierarchical fronts: F₁ (non-dominated), F₂ (non-dominated after removing F₁), F₃, etc. Time complexity: O(M · N²) where M is number of objectives. Ensures convergence toward the true Pareto front across generations.
Crowding Distance Operator Measures the density of neighbouring solutions around a specific point in objective space. Points with larger cuboid distances are in less crowded areas. Promotes uniform spread of solutions along the front without needing a user-specified sharing parameter (σshare).
Crowded-Comparison Operator (≺n) Between individuals i and j, i ≺n j if:
• rank(i) < rank(j) (lower front is better), OR
• rank(i) == rank(j) AND crowding_distance(i) > crowding_distance(j). 		Simultaneously drives solutions toward the Pareto front and spreads them evenly.
Elitist Replacement (2N → N) Combines parent population P_t and offspring Q_t into a pool of size 2N. Fills P_{t+1} front by front (F₁, F₂, ...) until N slots are occupied. Guarantees that previous high-quality trade-offs are never discarded.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

1. When is solution A said to “dominate” solution B in multi-objective optimization?

This is the rigorous mathematical definition of Pareto dominance (A ≺ B).

2. In NSGA-II, what purpose does the “crowding distance” parameter serve?

Crowding distance estimates the perimeter of the cuboid formed by nearest neighbours; higher distance means lower local crowding, preventing clustering.

3. In the worked GA numerical for f(x) = x², why did chromosome S₂ (11000) receive 2 copies in the mating pool?

Fitness-proportionate selection allocates expected copies e_i = f_i / f̄ = 576 / 292.5 ≈ 1.97, meaning it statistically wins roughly two spins.

Recall without looking

Write down the roulette wheel selection probability and expected count formulas.

Selection probability: P_i = f_i / Σf_j. Expected count: e_i = f_i / f̄ (where f̄ = average population fitness).

What is the Pareto front?

The set of all non-dominated solutions plotted in the objective space. Moving along the Pareto front requires trading off improvement in one objective for degradation in another.

Name the three defining mechanisms of NSGA-II.

1. Fast non-dominated sorting (O(M·N²)). 2. Crowding distance computation for diversity preservation. 3. Elitist (2N → N) selection merging parent and child pools.
