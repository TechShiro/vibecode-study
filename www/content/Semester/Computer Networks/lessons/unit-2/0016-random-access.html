Multiple Access: Random Access

ALOHA, CSMA, CSMA/CD and CSMA/CA. Two throughput formulas, the three persistence methods, the minimum-frame-size rule, and the reason wireless cannot use the same mechanism as wired Ethernet.

Exam strategy

Three near-certain questions. (1) Pure vs slotted ALOHA with the throughput formulas and their maxima — memorise 0.184 at G = 0.5 and 0.368 at G = 1; those two numbers alone often carry the question. (2) The three persistence methods, with a flowchart for each. (3) CSMA/CD vs CSMA/CA, and why wireless needs avoidance rather than detection — that "why" is where the marks separate.

The three families of multiple access

When many stations share one link, something must decide who transmits when. The Medium Access Control (MAC) sublayer of the data link layer does this, and its protocols fall into three families:

FamilyPrincipleProtocols
Random access (contention)No station is superior; each transmits when it wishes, and collisions are possibleALOHA, CSMA, CSMA/CD, CSMA/CA — this lesson
Controlled accessStations consult one another to find which has the right to send — no collisionsReservation, polling, token passing — Lesson 17
ChannelizationThe available bandwidth is shared out in time, frequency or codeFDMA, TDMA, CDMA — Lesson 17

In random access, two features define the family: there is no scheduled time for a station to transmit, and no rules specify which station sends next. Stations compete, so the protocols must answer four questions: when can a station access the medium, what if the medium is busy, how can a station know if its transmission succeeded, and what to do if there is a collision.

ALOHA

The earliest random access method, developed at the University of Hawaii for a radio network. Its rule is as simple as possible: whenever a station has a frame, it sends it. If an acknowledgement does not arrive within the timeout, the station assumes a collision and resends after a random back-off time.

Pure ALOHA

  • Stations transmit whenever they like, with no coordination at all.
  • A frame is destroyed if any part of it overlaps with any part of another frame.
  • Vulnerable time = 2 × Tfr — a collision occurs if another station sends within one frame-time before or one frame-time after our transmission starts.

Slotted ALOHA

  • Time is divided into slots of Tfr seconds, and a station must wait for the beginning of a slot to transmit.
  • This halves the danger window: two stations either choose the same slot (collision) or different slots (both succeed).
  • Vulnerable time = Tfr — exactly half that of pure ALOHA, which is why the throughput doubles.
ALOHA throughput Pure ALOHA:   S = G × e−2G   →   maximum S = 0.184 at G = 0.5 (18.4%)
Slotted ALOHA:   S = G × e−G   →   maximum S = 0.368 at G = 1 (36.8%)
G = average number of frames generated per frame-time  ·  S = throughput
The four numbers to memorise

Pure ALOHA: 18.4% at G = 0.5. Slotted ALOHA: 36.8% at G = 1. Slotted is exactly twice as good, because slotting halves the vulnerable time. If you remember nothing else about ALOHA, remember these.

Worked numerical — ALOHA throughput

Q. A shared 200 kbps channel is used by 200 stations, each sending 200-bit frames. Each station sends on average one frame per second. Find the throughput for pure and for slotted ALOHA.

Frame time Tfr = 200 bits ÷ 200,000 bps = 1 ms

Load G = frames generated per frame-time = 200 stations × 1 frame/s × 0.001 s = 0.2

Pure ALOHA: S = G e−2G = 0.2 × e−0.4 = 0.2 × 0.6703 = 0.134
Successful frames per second = 0.134 ÷ 0.001 = 134 frames/s out of 200 attempted.

Slotted ALOHA: S = G e−G = 0.2 × e−0.2 = 0.2 × 0.8187 = 0.164
Successful frames per second = 164 frames/s.

Sanity check: slotted must always beat pure at the same G, and 164 > 134. ✓ Also note that both are well below the maxima, because G = 0.2 is below the optimal load — the channel is under-used rather than congested.

CSMA — Carrier Sense Multiple Access

ALOHA transmits blindly. CSMA adds one rule: "listen before talking" (sense before transmit). A station first checks whether the medium is idle.

Collisions still happen, and the reason is the exam's favourite point: propagation delay. When station A begins transmitting, the first bit takes time to reach station B. If B senses the channel during that interval, it hears silence, concludes the medium is free, and transmits — producing a collision. Hence:

CSMA vulnerable time Vulnerable time = Tp, the maximum propagation time
The longer the propagation time, the worse the chance of collision.

The three persistence methods

MethodIf the channel is idleIf the channel is busyTrade-off
1-persistentTransmit immediately (with probability 1)Keep sensing continuously, and transmit the instant it becomes idleHighest chance of collision — if several stations are waiting, they all pounce at the same moment. Used by Ethernet.
Non-persistentTransmit immediatelyWait a random amount of time, then sense againReduces collisions, but wastes bandwidth — the channel may sit idle while everyone is in a random wait.
p-persistentTransmit with probability p; with probability 1−p, wait one time slot and repeatKeep sensing until idle, then apply the p ruleThe compromise — reduces collisions and improves efficiency. Used on slotted channels.

CSMA/CD — Collision Detection

CSMA says what to do before sending but nothing about what happens after a collision. CSMA/CD adds: monitor the channel while transmitting, and if a collision is detected, abort immediately.

The procedure

  1. Sense the channel; when idle, begin transmitting (1-persistent, for Ethernet).
  2. While transmitting, keep listening. A collision is detected as an abnormal rise in signal energy on the medium.
  3. On detecting a collision, abort the transmission at once and send a short jam signal so every station knows a collision occurred.
  4. Wait a random back-off time, then return to step 1.
The minimum frame size rule — asked as a numerical

A station must still be transmitting when the collision signal gets back to it, otherwise it will never learn of the collision. So the transmission must last at least the round-trip propagation time:

Minimum frame size for CSMA/CD Tfr ≥ 2 × Tp
Minimum frame length Lmin = 2 × Tp × Bandwidth
Worked numerical — minimum frame size

Q. A 10 Mbps CSMA/CD network has a maximum one-way propagation time of 25.6 µs. Find the minimum frame size.

Round-trip time = 2 × Tp = 2 × 25.6 = 51.2 µs (this is Ethernet's slot time)

Lmin = 51.2 × 10−6 s × 10 × 106 bps = 512 bits = 64 bytes

This is exactly the 64-byte minimum frame size of standard Ethernet — say so; it converts a calculation into an explanation, and it is usually worth a mark.

The consequence worth adding: as bandwidth rises, the minimum frame size must rise too, or the network's maximum length must shrink. That is why Gigabit Ethernet needs carrier extension to pad short frames.

Binary exponential back-off

After the kth collision, the station waits a random number of slot times chosen from 0 to 2k − 1. So after one collision it picks from {0, 1}; after two, from {0, 1, 2, 3}; after three, from {0…7}. The range doubles each time, spreading the contenders further apart as congestion grows. After 16 attempts the frame is discarded and the failure reported.

CSMA/CA — Collision Avoidance

The wireless counterpart. The question examiners ask is why a different mechanism is needed at all, and there are three reasons:

ReasonExplanation
Detection needs full duplexTo detect a collision, a station must send and receive at the same time. Wireless stations are typically half-duplex — they cannot listen while transmitting.
Signal attenuationA radio signal fades rapidly with distance. The transmitting station's own signal drowns out the far weaker signal of a distant colliding station, so a collision would be inaudible even if it could listen.
Hidden station problemTwo stations may both be in range of the access point but not in range of each other. Neither can sense the other's transmission at all, so carrier sensing does not prevent their collision.

The three avoidance strategies

StrategyHow it works
IFS — Interframe SpaceWhen the channel is found idle, the station does not send immediately; it waits an interframe space first. This allows for a distant station's signal still in flight, and the length of the IFS sets priority — a shorter IFS means higher priority.
Contention windowAfter the IFS, the station waits a random number of slots from a window whose size doubles after each collision (binary exponential back-off). The station freezes its timer if the channel becomes busy and resumes when it is idle again — so a station that has already waited a long time keeps its advantage.
AcknowledgementBecause collisions cannot be detected, success is confirmed positively: the receiver returns an ACK. No ACK within the timeout means the frame is presumed lost, and it is resent.

RTS/CTS handshake — the optional extension that solves the hidden station problem. Before sending, a station transmits a short Request To Send; the access point replies with a Clear To Send, which all stations in its range hear, including those hidden from the sender. They then defer for the announced duration. Mention this in one line; it is often the last mark on a CSMA/CA question.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

1. The maximum throughput of pure ALOHA and slotted ALOHA are respectively:

S = Ge^(−2G) peaks at 0.184 when G = 0.5; S = Ge^(−G) peaks at 0.368 when G = 1. Slotting halves the vulnerable time from 2Tfr to Tfr, which is exactly why the throughput doubles.

2. Collisions still occur in CSMA despite carrier sensing because:

The first bit of A's frame takes time to reach B. During that window B senses silence, believes the medium free, and transmits. That is why the vulnerable time of CSMA equals the maximum propagation time Tp.

3. Which persistence method waits a random time when it finds the channel busy?

Non-persistent stops sensing and waits a random period, reducing collisions but leaving the channel idle sometimes. 1-persistent keeps sensing and pounces the instant it clears — which is why several waiting stations collide.

4. A 100 Mbps CSMA/CD network has a one-way propagation time of 5 μs. The minimum frame size is:

Lmin = 2 × Tp × bandwidth = 10 μs × 10⁸ = 1000 bits. Forgetting to double Tp is the usual error — the station must still be sending when the collision signal returns, so the round trip is what matters.

5. Wireless networks use CSMA/CA rather than CSMA/CD mainly because:

Detection requires sending and listening simultaneously, which half-duplex radios cannot do; and signal attenuation means a far-off colliding signal is far weaker than the station's own. Add the hidden station problem and detection becomes unworkable — hence avoidance.

6. After its third successive collision, a station using binary exponential back-off waits a random number of slots chosen from:

The range after the kth collision is 0 to 2^k − 1, so k = 3 gives 0 to 7. The range doubles each time, spreading contenders further apart as congestion rises. After 16 attempts the frame is discarded.

Recall without looking

Name the three families of multiple access protocols with their members.

Random access (contention): ALOHA, CSMA, CSMA/CD, CSMA/CA. Controlled access: reservation, polling, token passing. Channelization: FDMA, TDMA, CDMA.

State the two ALOHA throughput formulas with their maxima.

Pure ALOHA: S = Ge^(−2G), maximum 0.184 (18.4%) at G = 0.5. Slotted ALOHA: S = Ge^(−G), maximum 0.368 (36.8%) at G = 1.

Give the vulnerable time of pure ALOHA, slotted ALOHA and CSMA.

Pure ALOHA: 2 × Tfr. Slotted ALOHA: Tfr. CSMA: Tp, the maximum propagation time.

Describe the three persistence methods.

1-persistent: transmit immediately when idle, and if busy keep sensing continuously and send the instant it clears — most collisions, used by Ethernet. Non-persistent: if busy, wait a random time then sense again — fewer collisions but wasted bandwidth. p-persistent: when idle transmit with probability p, else wait a slot and repeat — the compromise, used on slotted channels.

Describe the CSMA/CD procedure.

Sense the channel and transmit when idle; keep listening while transmitting; on detecting a collision (an abnormal rise in signal energy), abort immediately and send a jam signal; wait a random back-off time and retry. After 16 attempts, discard the frame.

State the minimum frame size rule and explain why it exists.

Tfr ≥ 2 × Tp, so Lmin = 2 × Tp × bandwidth. The station must still be transmitting when the collision signal reaches it back, or it would never learn of the collision. For 10 Mbps Ethernet this gives 512 bits = 64 bytes.

Why can wireless networks not use CSMA/CD? Give three reasons.

Collision detection needs full duplex, but wireless stations are half-duplex. Signals attenuate rapidly, so a distant colliding signal is drowned by the station's own. And the hidden station problem means two stations may be unable to sense each other at all.

Name the three strategies of CSMA/CA.

Interframe space (wait after finding the channel idle; its length sets priority), contention window (random back-off doubling after each collision, with the timer frozen while the channel is busy), and acknowledgement (positive confirmation, since collisions cannot be detected). Optionally RTS/CTS to solve the hidden station problem.
