Data Rate Limits and Performance Analysis

Nyquist and Shannon — two formulas, four worked numericals, and the one rule for deciding which to use. This is the most reliably scoring topic in Unit II because the answers are objectively right or wrong.

Exam strategy

A Nyquist or Shannon numerical appears in essentially every paper. Learn both formulas, learn the one-line rule for which applies, and always finish with the combined-use conclusion: when both are given, the Shannon limit is the ceiling and the Nyquist formula tells you how many signal levels to use to reach it. That last step is where most students stop early and lose marks.

The question both formulas answer

How fast can we send data over a given channel? Three factors decide it: the bandwidth available, the number of signal levels used, and the quality of the channel (its noise level).

The rule for choosing, in one line

If the question mentions SNR or noise, use Shannon. If it mentions signal levels, use Nyquist. If it gives both, use Shannon to find the ceiling and then Nyquist to find the number of levels needed.

Nyquist — the noiseless channel

Nyquist bit rate BitRate = 2 × B × log2 L
B = bandwidth in Hz  ·  L = number of signal levels

Two points that carry marks in a theory question:

  • Increasing the levels L increases the bit rate, but only logarithmically — doubling L adds one bit per level, not double the rate.
  • You cannot increase L indefinitely. The more levels there are, the closer together they sit, and the harder it becomes for the receiver to distinguish them in the presence of even slight noise. Increasing L beyond a point reduces reliability rather than raising throughput.
Worked numerical 1 — plain Nyquist

Q. A noiseless channel has a bandwidth of 3000 Hz. Find the maximum bit rate if the signal uses (a) 2 levels, (b) 4 levels.

(a) L = 2: BitRate = 2 × 3000 × log22 = 2 × 3000 × 1 = 6000 bps

(b) L = 4: BitRate = 2 × 3000 × log24 = 2 × 3000 × 2 = 12,000 bps

Sanity check: doubling the levels from 2 to 4 doubled the rate here only because log₂ went from 1 to 2. Going to 8 levels would give 18,000 bps — a 50% gain, not another doubling. The returns diminish, which is exactly the point Nyquist makes.

Worked numerical 2 — Nyquist run backwards

Q. We need to send 265 kbps over a noiseless channel of bandwidth 20 kHz. How many signal levels are required?

265,000 = 2 × 20,000 × log2L

log2L = 265,000 / 40,000 = 6.625

L = 26.625 ≈ 98.7 levels

Conclusion: the result is not a power of 2, and the number of levels must be. So this bit rate is not achievable on this channel — we must either increase the bandwidth or accept a lower rate. Say this explicitly; the "answer" to this question is the impossibility, not the number 98.7.

Shannon — the noisy channel

Shannon capacity Capacity = B × log2(1 + SNR)
B = bandwidth in Hz  ·  SNR = signal-to-noise ratio (a plain ratio, not in dB)

Three properties of the formula that make good theory answer points:

  • The number of signal levels does not appear. No matter how many levels you use, you cannot exceed the Shannon capacity.
  • Capacity is the highest data rate for a noisy channel; it is a bound on any conceivable encoding scheme.
  • If SNR = 0 (noise power equals signal power), capacity = B × log2(1) = 0. A channel that is pure noise carries nothing, however wide it is.
The unit trap that costs marks

If the question gives SNR in decibels, you must convert before substituting: SNR = 10(SNRdB/10). Substituting the dB value directly into Shannon's formula is the single most common error on this question. There is one shortcut worth knowing: for large SNR, Capacity ≈ B × SNRdB / 3.

Worked numerical 3 — Shannon with SNR in dB

Q. A telephone line has a bandwidth of 3000 Hz and an SNR of 3162. Find the capacity. Then repeat for a line of bandwidth 1 MHz with SNR of 63.

(a) C = 3000 × log2(1 + 3162) = 3000 × log2(3163)

log2(3163) ≈ 11.63

C = 3000 × 11.63 = 34,881 bps ≈ 34.8 kbps

This is why 33.6 kbps was the practical ceiling for telephone-line modems — a good closing line for the answer.

(b) C = 106 × log2(1 + 63) = 106 × log2(64) = 106 × 6 = 6 Mbps

Sanity check: when SNR + 1 is a power of 2, the log is exact and the answer is clean. Examiners choose 3, 7, 15, 31, 63, 127 for exactly this reason — if your SNR is one less than a power of two, you are on the intended path.

Worked numerical 4 — both formulas together (the full-marks question)

Q. A channel has a bandwidth of 1 MHz and an SNR of 63. What are the appropriate bit rate and signal level?

Step 1 — Shannon gives the ceiling:
C = 106 × log2(1 + 63) = 106 × 6 = 6 Mbps

Step 2 — choose a bit rate below the ceiling. Shannon's capacity is an upper bound that cannot be achieved in practice, so choose something lower, say 4 Mbps.

Step 3 — Nyquist gives the levels needed for that rate:
4 × 106 = 2 × 106 × log2L
log2L = 2  →  L = 4 signal levels

Answer: use 4 Mbps with 4 signal levels.

The examiner's point: Shannon tells you the limit; Nyquist tells you how to build a signal that reaches it. Write that sentence — it is the whole reason the two formulas are taught together.

Performance analysis

The syllabus lists "performance analysis" separately. It is the same four measures introduced in Unit I, and the same formulas — so this is revision rather than new material, but it is examinable in its own right.

The performance formulas Latency = Propagation + Transmission + Queuing + Processing
Propagation time = Distance ÷ Propagation speed
Transmission time = Message size ÷ Bandwidth
Bandwidth-delay product = Bandwidth × Delay
RTT (round-trip time) = 2 × Propagation time (plus any processing at the far end)

Worked numerical 5 — throughput

Q. A network with a bandwidth of 10 Mbps can pass an average of 12,000 frames per minute, each carrying 10,000 bits. Find the throughput, and comment.

Bits per minute = 12,000 × 10,000 = 1.2 × 108 bits

Throughput = 1.2 × 108 ÷ 60 = 2 Mbps

Comment: the throughput is 2 Mbps against a bandwidth of 10 Mbps — only one fifth of capacity. The network is far from being used to its potential. Always add this comparison sentence; it is usually worth a mark on its own.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

1. A question gives you bandwidth and SNR but no signal levels. Which formula applies?

SNR means noise, and noise means Shannon. Nyquist needs L; Shannon does not use L at all, which is exactly why it gives an absolute ceiling.

2. A channel has bandwidth 4 kHz and SNR = 3. Its Shannon capacity is:

C = B log₂(1 + SNR) = 4000 × log₂4 = 4000 × 2 = 8000 bps. Note there is no factor of 2 in Shannon's formula — that factor belongs to Nyquist. Mixing the two shapes is a common slip.

3. Doubling the number of signal levels from 4 to 8 changes the Nyquist bit rate by a factor of:

Bit rate ∝ log₂L, so going from L = 4 (log₂ = 2) to L = 8 (log₂ = 3) multiplies the rate by 3/2. This diminishing return is the substantive point of the Nyquist formula.

4. If a channel's noise power equals its signal power, its Shannon capacity is:

SNR is signal power ÷ noise power. Equal powers give SNR = 1, so C = B log₂(2) = B. But when SNR = 0 — noise swamping the signal entirely — C = B log₂(1) = 0. Read the SNR value carefully; the two cases differ.

5. A 10 Mbps network is measured carrying 2 Mbps. The correct statement is:

Capacity and bandwidth are what the channel offers; throughput is what is achieved. Latency is measured in seconds, never bits per second — a unit mismatch is always the giveaway.

Recall without looking

State the Nyquist bit rate formula and define each term.

BitRate = 2 × B × log₂L, for a noiseless channel. B is bandwidth in hertz, L is the number of signal levels.

State the Shannon capacity formula and its key property.

Capacity = B × log₂(1 + SNR), for a noisy channel. The number of signal levels does not appear — no encoding scheme can exceed this rate.

Why can we not raise the number of signal levels indefinitely?

The more levels there are, the closer together they sit, and the harder it becomes for the receiver to distinguish them in the presence of noise. Beyond a point, increasing L reduces reliability rather than raising throughput.

How do you use Nyquist and Shannon together?

Use Shannon to find the upper limit of the data rate, choose a rate somewhat below it, then use Nyquist to find how many signal levels are needed to carry that rate. Shannon gives the limit; Nyquist gives the signal design.

How do you convert an SNR given in decibels?

SNR = 10^(SNR_dB / 10). Never substitute a dB figure directly into Shannon's formula. Shortcut for large SNR: Capacity ≈ B × SNR_dB / 3.

Write the five performance formulas.

Latency = propagation + transmission + queuing + processing. Propagation = distance/speed. Transmission = size/bandwidth. BDP = bandwidth × delay. RTT = 2 × propagation time.
