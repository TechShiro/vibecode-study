Data Flow and Connection Types

Simplex, half-duplex and full-duplex — a named syllabus topic and a guaranteed short question. Plus point-to-point vs multipoint connections, serial vs parallel transmission, and the unicast/broadcast/multicast trio.

Exam strategy

"Explain the three modes of data flow with diagrams" is a 5-mark question that takes three minutes if you have the arrow diagrams memorised. Always draw the arrows — three tiny sketches score more reliably than three paragraphs. The keyboard/monitor, walkie-talkie and telephone examples are the ones examiners expect; use those, not invented ones.

Data flow: the three modes

Communication between two devices can be simplex, half-duplex or full-duplex. The distinction is simply which directions data can travel, and whether it can travel both ways at the same instant.

1. Simplex

Communication is unidirectional, as on a one-way street. Only one of the two devices on a link can transmit; the other can only receive.

Simplex A  ———→  B     (A can only send, B can only receive)

Example: keyboard and traditional monitor. The keyboard can only introduce input; the monitor can only accept output. Capacity: the entire capacity of the channel is used by the one transmitting device.

2. Half-duplex

Each station can both transmit and receive, but not at the same time. When one device is sending, the other can only receive, and vice versa. Like a one-lane road with traffic allowed in both directions — while cars travel one way, cars going the other way must wait.

Half-duplex A  ———→  B    (at time t1)
A  ←———  B    (at time t2)

Examples: walkie-talkies, CB radios. Capacity: the entire capacity of the channel is taken over by whichever device is transmitting at that moment.

Where it is used: when there is no need for communication in both directions at the same time.

3. Full-duplex (duplex)

Both stations can transmit and receive simultaneously. Like a two-way street with traffic flowing in both directions at once.

Full-duplex A  ———→  B
A  ←———  B    (both at the same time)

Example: the telephone network — both people can talk and be heard at once.

How it is achieved (this sub-point is often worth an extra mark): either the link contains two physically separate transmission paths, one for each direction, or the single channel's capacity is divided between signals travelling in opposite directions.

Capacity: if the channel is shared, the capacity must be divided between the two directions.

If you can only remember one line

Simplex = one direction. Half-duplex = both directions, taking turns. Full-duplex = both directions, at once. Everything else in the table follows from that.

Connection types: point-to-point vs multipoint

A network is two or more devices connected through links. A link is a communications pathway that transfers data from one device to another. There are exactly two possible types of connection.

BasisPoint-to-pointMultipoint (multidrop)
DefinitionA dedicated link between exactly two devicesMore than two devices share a single link
CapacityThe entire capacity of the link is reserved for those two devicesThe capacity of the channel is shared, either spatially or temporally
SharingNoneSpatially shared — several devices use the link at the same time. Time-shared — users take turns.
CostHigher — a separate link per pairLower — one link serves many
ExampleChanging TV channels with an infrared remote; a microwave link between two towersThe shared backbone of a bus topology

Point-to-point links are not always wires. A microwave or infrared link between just two devices is still point-to-point — the defining feature is that the link is dedicated to that pair, not that it is physical.

Serial vs parallel transmission

A short bonus topic that is sometimes tacked onto a data-flow question. Do not confuse it with the duplex modes — this is about how many bits travel at once, not in which direction.

BasisSerial transmissionParallel transmission
Bits sentOne bit at a time, over a single wiren bits at a time, over n separate wires
Wires neededOnen (one per bit)
SpeedSlower per clock cyclen times faster per clock cycle
CostLowHigh — n wires
DistanceSuitable for long distancesOnly short distances — skew and crosstalk between the wires grow with length
ExampleUSB, SATA, all network cablingOld printer (Centronics) port, internal computer buses

Serial transmission is further divided into asynchronous (start and stop bits, gaps between bytes), synchronous (a continuous stream of bits with no gaps, timed by a common clock) and isochronous (fixed, guaranteed timing between frames — used for real-time audio and video). One line each is enough.

Delivery modes: unicast, broadcast, multicast

The third small classification, and the one most often confused. Here the question is how many receivers.

ModeReceiversRelationshipExample
UnicastExactly oneOne-to-oneLoading a web page; sending a file to one machine
BroadcastEvery device on the networkOne-to-allARP request; a radio or TV transmission
MulticastA specific group of devices that chose to joinOne-to-manyLive video streaming to subscribers; IGMP groups
AnycastThe nearest one of a groupOne-to-nearestDNS root servers, CDN nodes
The distinction that gets marks

Broadcast goes to everyone whether they want it or not; multicast goes only to those who joined the group. That one sentence separates the two cleanly. Anycast is a bonus — mention it in one line and move on, it is rarely more than a mark.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

1. A walkie-talkie pair is the standard example of which data flow mode?

You press the button to talk and release it to listen — you cannot do both at once. That is the definition of half-duplex, and this exact example is the one the textbook uses.

2. In a full-duplex link that uses a single shared channel, the channel capacity is:

Full duplex needs either two physically separate paths, or one channel whose capacity is split between the directions. Capacity is never created by the mode — only allocated differently.

3. Several devices share one backbone cable, taking turns to use it. This connection is:

More than two devices on a single link is multipoint by definition. "Taking turns" makes it time-shared rather than spatially shared. Note that only one device transmitting at a time does not make it simplex — simplex is about fixed direction, not turns.

4. Parallel transmission is limited to short distances mainly because:

Over distance, the n wires no longer deliver their bits at exactly the same instant (skew) and interfere with each other (crosstalk). That is why every long-distance link, and every network cable, is serial.

5. A live stream is delivered only to the users who subscribed to it. This is:

Broadcast reaches everyone whether they asked or not. Multicast reaches only those who joined the group — which is exactly what "subscribed" means here.

Recall without looking

Define the three data flow modes with one example each.

Simplex — unidirectional; only one device transmits (keyboard → monitor). Half-duplex — both directions but not at the same time (walkie-talkie). Full-duplex — both directions simultaneously (telephone).

How is full-duplex physically achieved?

Either the link has two physically separate transmission paths, one per direction, or the capacity of a single channel is divided between signals travelling in opposite directions.

Difference between point-to-point and multipoint connection?

Point-to-point: a dedicated link between exactly two devices; the entire capacity is reserved for them. Multipoint: more than two devices share a single link; capacity is shared either spatially (used at the same time) or temporally (users take turns).

Serial vs parallel transmission — three differences.

Serial sends one bit at a time on one wire, is cheaper, and works over long distances. Parallel sends n bits at a time on n wires, is n times faster per cycle, costs more, and is limited to short distances by skew and crosstalk.

Distinguish broadcast from multicast in one sentence.

Broadcast delivers to every device on the network whether it wants the data or not; multicast delivers only to the specific group of devices that joined.
