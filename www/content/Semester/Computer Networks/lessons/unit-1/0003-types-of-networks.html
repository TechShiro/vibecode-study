Types of Networks: PAN, LAN, MAN, WAN

Networks classified by geographic span, then by architecture. One comparison table carries most of the marks here — the rest is vocabulary you must not mix up: internet vs Internet, intranet vs extranet.

Exam strategy

"Differentiate between LAN, MAN and WAN" is close to guaranteed — a 5-mark table. Give it six or seven rows: span, ownership, speed, error rate, cost, technology, example. Add PAN as a fourth column only if the question names it. Then keep two spare lines for client-server vs peer-to-peer, which is asked separately just as often.

Classification by geographic span

A fifth term, CAN (Campus Area Network), appears in some question papers: several LANs of one campus interconnected, sitting between LAN and MAN in scale. One line is enough.

LAN — the points worth writing

  • Privately owned, linking devices in a single office, building or campus.
  • Size is limited by design, so the worst-case transmission time is bounded and known in advance — this is the property that lets LAN protocols be simple and efficient. Worth stating; it is a real distinguishing point, not padding.
  • All devices on a LAN share a common transmission medium, and each has a unique MAC address.
  • Common topologies: star (modern, using a switch) and bus (older).

MAN — the points worth writing

  • Spans a town or city; larger than a LAN, smaller than a WAN.
  • Typically interconnects a number of LANs spread across the city.
  • Often owned and operated by a single body, such as a cable television operator or an ISP, but shared by many users.
  • Examples: a city-wide cable TV network, a telephone company's DSL network, an organisation with offices across one city linked together.

WAN — the points worth writing

  • Spans a large geographical area — a country, a continent, or the whole world.
  • Interconnects LANs and MANs.
  • Usually not owned by one organisation; links are leased from telecommunication providers.
  • Two sub-types, which is a common 2-mark add-on:
    • Point-to-point WAN — connects two communicating devices through a single transmission medium such as a leased line.
    • Switched WAN — a network with more than two endpoints, built from switches connecting many point-to-point WANs. The Internet backbone is a switched WAN.

Internetwork, internet and the Internet

This is a vocabulary trap, and it is easy marks if you get the capital letter right.

TermMeaning
Internetwork (internet), small iAny two or more networks connected together. Generic — there are millions of internets.
The Internet, capital IOne specific, global internetwork — the worldwide public network of networks that grew out of ARPANET. There is exactly one.
IntranetA private network that uses Internet technology (TCP/IP, browsers, web servers) but is accessible only inside one organisation. Example: a company's internal portal.
ExtranetAn intranet partially extended to authorised outsiders — suppliers, customers, partners — usually over a secured connection.
One-line answer

An internet is any network of networks; the Internet is the one everybody uses. An intranet is private to one organisation; an extranet lets specific outsiders in.

Classification by architecture

The second way to classify networks: not by how far they reach, but by how the devices on them relate to each other.

CostHigher — needs dedicated server hardwareLower — no dedicated server
ScalabilityServer becomes a bottleneck as clients growScales well — every new peer adds capacity

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

1. Which property of a LAN follows directly from its size being limited by design?

Because the maximum distance is fixed, the maximum delay is fixed too. That bound is what allows LAN protocols to be far simpler than WAN protocols — a genuinely distinguishing point, and a good line to include in any LAN answer.

2. A cable television network covering one city is the standard example of a:

City-sized, typically operator-owned, interconnecting many local networks. This is the textbook MAN example, so it is the safest one to write.

3. A company portal reachable only by its own employees, built on TCP/IP and web browsers, is an:

Intranet = same technology as the Internet, but access confined inside the organisation. It becomes an extranet only once outsiders such as suppliers are deliberately let in.

4. The main reliability weakness of the client-server architecture is that:

Centralisation buys control and security, and pays for it with a single point of failure and a scaling bottleneck. P2P has the opposite profile.

5. Which type of WAN connects more than two endpoints using switches?

A point-to-point WAN joins exactly two devices over one medium. A switched WAN has many endpoints and is effectively a mesh of point-to-point WANs — the Internet backbone is the example.

Recall without looking

Give five points of difference between LAN and WAN.

Span (building/campus vs country or world); ownership (private vs usually leased from operators); speed (high, 100 Mbps–10 Gbps vs comparatively low); error rate and congestion (low vs high); cost and maintenance (low and easy vs high and difficult). Technology: Ethernet/Wi-Fi vs leased lines, MPLS, satellite.

What is a MAN and who typically owns one?

A network spanning a town or city, usually interconnecting several LANs. Often owned and operated by a single body such as a cable TV operator or ISP, but shared by many users. Example: a city cable television network.

Name the two types of WAN.

Point-to-point WAN — connects two devices through a single transmission medium such as a leased line. Switched WAN — more than two endpoints, built from switches joining many point-to-point WANs; the Internet backbone is one.

internet vs Internet vs intranet vs extranet.

internet (small i) = any two or more networks joined. Internet (capital I) = the one global public internetwork. Intranet = private network using Internet technology, inside one organisation. Extranet = an intranet partly opened to authorised outsiders.

Client-server vs peer-to-peer — four differences.

Roles (fixed vs every node is both); storage (centralised vs distributed); reliability (server is a single point of failure vs none); security and administration (strong and central vs weak and per-peer). Also cost and scalability.
