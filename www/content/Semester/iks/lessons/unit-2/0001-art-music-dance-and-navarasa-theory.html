Art, Music, Dance & The Navarasa Theory

The Nāṭyaśāstra of Bharata Muni, the 64 Kalās, the science of aesthetics (Rasa), and sacred architecture.

1 · The Nāṭyaśāstra: The Fifth Veda (Nāṭya Veda)

In the Bhāratīya worldview, art, dance, and music were never mere sensory entertainment; they were recognized as sacred pathways to spiritual elevation and psychological catharsis. The foundational encyclopedia of Indian performing arts is the Nāṭyaśāstra, attributed to Sage Bharata Muni (c. 500 BCE – 200 CE).

Genesis of the Nāṭya Veda

According to tradition, Brahmā created the Nāṭya Veda by synthesizing elements from the four Vedas:

  • Pāṭhya (Recitation & Dialogue) from the Ṛgveda
  • Gāna (Melodic Chanting & Music) from the Sāmaveda
  • Abhinaya (Histrionic Expression & Gesture) from the Yajurveda
  • Rasa (Aesthetic Emotion & Sentiment) from the Atharvaveda

2 · The Four Dimensions of Expression: Caturvidha Abhinaya

An actor, dancer, or musician communicates with the audience through four interconnected modes of expression:

1. Āṅgika Abhinaya (Body)

Physical gestures, hand mudrās, facial expressions, eye movements (*Dṛṣṭi-bheda*), and postures (*Karaṇas* and *Cārīs*).

2. Vācika Abhinaya (Voice)

Vocal modulation, poetic rhythm, diction, pronunciation, singing, and dialogue delivery.

3. Āhārya Abhinaya (Costume)

Stage decor, ornaments, costumes, symbolic makeup, masks, and mechanical props.

4. Sāttvika Abhinaya (Soul)

Genuine inner emotional states expressed through involuntary physiological responses: tears, trembling, goosebumps, sweating.

3 · The Navarasa Theory (The Nine Aesthetic Sentiments)

The crown jewel of Indian aesthetic theory is the Rasa Theory. Rasa literally translates to juice, essence, or taste. When an audience experiences a performance, permanent human psychological emotions (Sthāyī Bhāvas) are transformed into elevated, universal aesthetic delights:

4 · The 64 Kalās (Catuḥṣaṣṭi Kalās)

Ancient Bhārata did not artificially divide "fine arts" from "engineering skills". In texts like the Kāmasūtra and Śukranīti, an educated citizen was expected to attain proficiency across 64 practical and creative arts (Kalās). These included:

  • Performing Arts: Vocal music (*Gītam*), instrumental music (*Vādyam*), dance (*Nṛtyam*), theater (*Nāṭakam*).
  • Visual Arts: Painting (*Alekhyam*), clay modeling, sculpture (*Mūrtikalā*).
  • Applied Sciences & Engineering: Architecture (*Vāstuvidyā*), gemological testing (*Rūpyaratnaparīkṣā*), metallurgy (*Dhātuvāda*), gardening and botanical grafting (*Vṛkṣāyurveda*).
  • Linguistics & Cryptography: Secret code writing (*Mlecchita-vikalpa*), poetry composition, riddle solving.

5 · Indian Classical Music: Saṅgīta Ratnākara

Composed in the 13th century by Śārṅgadeva, the Saṅgīta Ratnākara is the definitive bridge connecting ancient music to both Northern (Hindustani) and Southern (Carnatic) classical traditions. It formalizes: Svara (the 7 natural notes: Sa, Re, Ga, Ma, Pa, Dha, Ni), Śruti (the 22 microtonal intervals), Rāga (melodic frameworks), and Tāla (cyclical rhythmic matrices).
