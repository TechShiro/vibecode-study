B-Trees and B+ Trees

Why disks need a different tree, the order rules, insertion by splitting, deletion by borrowing and merging, the height bounds, and the B-tree versus B+ tree comparison.

Exam strategy

“Construct a B-tree of order m by inserting the following keys” is the question, and it is entirely mechanical once you know two numbers: max keys = m − 1 and min keys = ⌈m/2⌉ − 1. Write both numbers at the top of your answer before you start. Redraw the tree after every split — the marks are in the intermediate states. Deletion is asked less often but follows one rule: borrow if a sibling can spare a key, otherwise merge. B-tree vs B+ tree is a reliable short comparison.

Why B-trees exist

The motivating sentence — open every B-tree answer with it

A disk access is roughly a hundred thousand times slower than a memory access. So for data stored on disk, the cost of a search is the number of nodes visited, not the number of comparisons. A binary tree of a million keys is about 20 levels deep — 20 disk reads. A B-tree makes each node as large as one disk block, so it holds hundreds of keys, and a million keys fit in two or three levels. B-trees minimise the height by maximising the branching factor.

The rules

A B-tree of order m 1. Every node has at most m children and therefore at most m − 1 keys.
2. Every node other than the root and the leaves has at least ⌈m/2⌉ children, hence at least ⌈m/2⌉ − 1 keys.
3. The root has at least 2 children (and at least 1 key), unless it is a leaf.
4. All leaves appear at the same level — the tree is perfectly height-balanced.
5. A node with k keys has exactly k + 1 children, and the keys within a node are kept in sorted order.

Note the important special case: a 2–3–4 tree (order 4) is exactly equivalent to a red–black tree. Every red–black tree can be converted into one and back — a fact worth stating when the paper asks you to relate the two.

Insertion — always at a leaf, always splitting upward

The algorithm Step 1. Search down to the correct leaf and insert the key there, keeping the node sorted. Keys are only ever inserted into leaves.
Step 2. If the leaf now holds more than m − 1 keys, it overflows. Then:
    SPLIT it into two nodes and push the MEDIAN key up into the parent.
Step 3. If the parent now overflows, split it too — and repeat upward.
Step 4. If the root splits, the median becomes a new root, and the height of the tree increases by one.
The two facts examiners test

1. A B-tree grows upward from the root, never downward from the leaves — which is exactly why all leaves stay at the same level.

2. With an even number of keys after overflow, take the LEFT median — for example, from 5 keys the 3rd is the median; from 4 keys after overflow in an order-4 tree, take the 2nd. State your convention; both are accepted, but be consistent.

Q. Construct a B-tree of order 5 by inserting 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 70, 80, 90.

Order 5 ⇒ maximum 4 keys per node, minimum 2 keys (except the root). Write that line first.

[ 10 | 20 | 30 | 40 ]  — full, but legal

  • The height grows from 0 to 1.

Note how flat it is: 9 keys in 2 levels. A binary search tree would need 4 levels; an AVL tree, 3.

Deletion — borrow, or merge

The algorithm Step 1. If the key is in an internal node, replace it with its inorder predecessor or successor (which lives in a leaf), and delete that instead. All real deletion happens at leaves.
Step 2. Remove the key from the leaf. If the leaf still has at least ⌈m/2⌉ − 1 keys, stop.
Step 3 — UNDERFLOW. Otherwise:
    (a) BORROW, if an immediate sibling has more than the minimum. Move the parent's separator key down into the deficient node, and move the sibling's adjacent key up into the parent. This is a rotation.
    (b) MERGE, if no sibling can spare a key. Combine the deficient node, the parent's separator key, and a sibling into one node. The parent loses a key and may itself underflow — repeat upward.
Step 4. If the root loses its last key, its single remaining child becomes the new root and the height decreases by one.
Q. From the final tree above, delete 40, then delete 50.

Delete 40. It is in the leaf [40 | 50], which becomes [50] — only 1 key, and the minimum is 2. UNDERFLOW.

  • Left sibling [10 | 20] has exactly 2 keys — the minimum, so it cannot spare one.
  • Right sibling [70 | 80 | 90] has 3 keys — it can lend.
  • Borrow from the right: the separator 60 comes down into the deficient node, and the sibling's smallest key 70 goes up to replace it.

The one-line rule to remember: “Borrow if you can, merge if you must.”

Height and complexity

The bounds for a B-tree of order m with n keys and height h Maximum keys at height h:   mh+1 − 1   (every node completely full)
Minimum keys at height h:   2⌈m/2⌉h − 1   (every node at the minimum)

Height bound:   h ≤ log⌈m/2⌉ ((n + 1) / 2)   ⇒   h = O(logm n)
Numerical. A B-tree of order 101 stores 106 keys. How many disk accesses does a search need in the worst case?

Minimum children per non-root node = ⌈101/2⌉ = 51.

Worst case: h ≤ log51((106 + 1)/2) = log51(500000) ≈ ln 500000 / ln 51 = 13.12 / 3.93 ≈ 3.34, so h ≤ 3.

Reading a path from the root visits h + 1 = 4 nodes, so at most 4 disk accesses.

Compare a balanced binary tree: log2(106) ≈ 20 levels → 20 disk accesses. Five times fewer disk reads, which is precisely why every database index is a B-tree or a B+ tree.

B+ trees

The two changes 1. All the actual data records live in the LEAVES. Internal nodes hold only keys, as separators for routing — a key may appear both in an internal node and again in a leaf.
2. The leaves are LINKED together in a singly or doubly linked list, in sorted order.

The sentence to end a comparison with

B+ trees dominate in practice because databases run range queries constantly — “all rows with salary between X and Y” — and the linked leaves turn that into one search plus a sequential scan. A plain B-tree would need a full traversal.

“Borrow if you can, merge if you must.” Borrowing rotates the parent's separator down and the sibling's adjacent key up; merging shrinks the parent and may cascade.

Recall without looking

Why do B-trees exist?

A disk access is about a hundred thousand times slower than a memory access, so the cost of a search is the number of nodes visited. Making each node the size of a disk block gives a branching factor in the hundreds, so a million keys sit in two or three levels rather than twenty. B-trees minimise height by maximising branching factor.

Give the five rules of a B-tree of order m.

At most m children and m − 1 keys per node; at least ⌈m/2⌉ children and ⌈m/2⌉ − 1 keys for every non-root, non-leaf node; the root has at least 2 children unless it is a leaf; all leaves are at the same level; and a node with k keys has exactly k + 1 children, kept sorted.

Describe B-tree insertion.

Insert only at a leaf, in sorted position. On overflow — more than m − 1 keys — split the node and push the median key up into the parent, repeating upward. If the root splits, the median becomes a new root and the height grows by one. The tree grows upward, which is why leaves stay level.

Describe B-tree deletion.

If the key is in an internal node, replace it with its inorder predecessor or successor from a leaf and delete that instead. On underflow, borrow from an immediate sibling with more than the minimum by rotating the parent's separator down and the sibling's key up; if no sibling can spare one, merge the node, the separator and a sibling into one, which may make the parent underflow in turn. If the root loses its last key the height decreases by one. “Borrow if you can, merge if you must.”

Give the height bounds.

Maximum keys at height h: m^(h+1) − 1. Minimum keys: 2⌈m/2⌉^h − 1. Height h ≤ log_⌈m/2⌉((n+1)/2), so h = O(log_m n). All operations cost Θ(log_m n) disk accesses.

Compare B-trees with B+ trees on six points.

Data in every node versus leaves only; no key duplication versus keys duplicated in internal nodes and leaves; leaves unlinked versus linked in sorted order; variable search cost versus uniform; poor versus excellent range queries; lower versus higher branching factor and so a shallower tree. B+ trees also make deletion easier, since it only happens in leaves, and they are what nearly every database index uses.
