AVL Trees

The balance factor, the four rotations with the rule for picking the right one, a full six-key insertion trace, deletion with its R0/R1/R−1 cases, and the minimum-nodes recurrence that bounds the height.

Exam strategy

“Construct an AVL tree by inserting the following keys, showing all rotations” is a 10-mark question and appears constantly. Three things earn the marks: write the balance factor beside every node after every insertion; name the rotation (LL, RR, LR, RL) before performing it; and redraw the tree after each rotation, not just at the end. The minimum-nodes recurrence N(h) = N(h−1) + N(h−2) + 1 is a separate short question worth memorising outright.

The definition

AVL tree — Adelson-Velsky and Landis, 1962 An AVL tree is a self-balancing binary search tree in which, for every node,

Balance Factor (BF) = height(left subtree) − height(right subtree) ∈ {−1, 0, +1}

Convention: the height of an empty subtree is −1, and the height of a single node is 0.
Some books use 0 for empty and 1 for a single node — the balance factors come out identical, so either is fine, but say which you use.
BFMeaningStatus
+1Left subtree is taller by oneBalanced
0Both subtrees have equal heightBalanced
−1Right subtree is taller by oneBalanced
+2 or −2Out of balanceRotate

It is the first self-balancing BST ever invented, and it guarantees h = O(log n), so search, insert and delete are all O(log n) in the worst case — unlike a plain BST, which degrades to O(n).

The four rotations

How to pick the rotation — the rule that removes all guesswork

1. After inserting, walk up from the new node and find the lowest node whose BF became ±2. Call it A.
2. Look at the first two steps of the path from A down to the newly inserted node.
3. Those two letters name the case: Left-Left, Right-Right, Left-Right or Right-Left.

LL and RR are “same direction twice” → a SINGLE rotation. LR and RL are “direction changes” → a DOUBLE rotation.

RLBF(A) = −2 and BF(right child) = +1Right rotation at the right child, then left rotation at A2

RL is the mirror image: right-rotate the right child first, then left-rotate at A.
The shortcut that saves time under exam pressure

Whatever the case, the outcome is always the same: of the three keys involved, the MIDDLE one becomes the subtree root, the smallest becomes its left child and the largest its right child. Write the three keys in sorted order and you have the answer — then justify it by naming the rotation.

Insertion — the full worked trace

Q. Build an AVL tree by inserting 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 25. Show every balance factor and every rotation.

Insert 10. Single node, BF = 0. Balanced.

Insert 20. Goes right of 10. BF(10) = 0 − 1 = −1. Balanced.

Insert 30. Goes right of 20. Now BF(20) = −1, and BF(10) = −2 — unbalanced.

Lowest unbalanced node A = 10. Path from 10 to 30: right, right → RR case → single left rotation at 10.

    20 (0)
   /   \
10(0)  30(0)

Insert 40. 40 > 20 → right; 40 > 30 → right of 30. BF(30) = −1, BF(20) = 1 − 2 = −1. Balanced — no rotation.

Insert 50. 50 > 20 → right; > 30 → right; > 40 → right of 40.

BF(40) = −1; BF(30) = 0 − 2 = −2 — unbalanced; (BF(20) = 1 − 3 = −2 too, but 30 is the LOWEST unbalanced node, so we fix 30).

Path from 30 to 50: right, right → RR → single left rotation at 30.

       20 (−1)
     /      \
 10(0)     40(0)
          /    \
       30(0)  50(0)

Note: fixing the lowest unbalanced node restored balance everywhere above it too. For an insertion, one rotation is always enough — a fact worth stating, because it is exactly what distinguishes insertion from deletion.

Insert 25. 25 > 20 → right; 25 < 40 → left; 25 < 30 → left of 30.

BF(30) = +1; BF(40) = 2 − 1 = +1; BF(20) = 1 − 3 = −2 — unbalanced.

A = 20. Path from 20 to 25: right (to 40), then left (to 30) → RL case → double rotation.

Step 1 — right rotation at the right child, 40. Node 30 rises; 40 becomes 30's right child.

Deletion

Delete exactly as in a plain BST (Lesson 7's three cases), then walk back up to the root, recomputing balance factors and rotating wherever one reaches ±2.

The two differences from insertion — both are marked

1. A deletion may need MORE THAN ONE rotation. An insertion needs at most one, because a single rotation restores the subtree's original height. A deletion reduces the height, so the imbalance can propagate all the way to the root — up to O(log n) rotations.

2. The rotation is chosen from the SIBLING's balance factor, not from the path to the deleted node — because the deleted node is gone. This is what the R0, R1, R−1, L0, L1, L−1 notation is for.

CaseBF(A)BF of A's left childRotation
L1+2+1Single right (like LL)
L0+20Single right (like LL) — occurs only on deletion
L−1+2−1Double: left then right (like LR)
CaseBF(A)BF of A's right childRotation
R−1−2−1Single left (like RR)
R0−20Single left (like RR) — occurs only on deletion
R1−2+1Double: right then left (like RL)

L0 and R0 cannot arise from an insertion at all — that is the whole reason this second notation exists. Mentioning it is worth a mark.

Q. Delete 10 from the final tree above.

Tree before: 30 as root; left subtree 20 with children 10 and 25; right subtree 40 with right child 50.

Step 1 — BST deletion. 10 is a leaf, so remove it.

Step 2 — recompute balance factors from the deletion point upward.

  • BF(20) = height(NULL) − height(25) = (−1) − 0 = −1. Fine.
  • BF(30) = height(20's subtree) − height(40's subtree) = 1 − 1 = 0. Fine.

No rotation is needed. The tree remains a valid AVL tree:

       30 (0)
     /      \
 20(−1)   40(−1)
   \           \
    25(0)    50(0)

Now delete 50 as well: BF(40) becomes 0 − (−1) = +1... wait, 40 now has no children at all, so BF(40) = 0, and BF(30) = 2 − 1 = +1. Still balanced — no rotation.

Now delete 25 too: 20 becomes a leaf, so BF(20) = 0; BF(30) = 1 − 1 = 0. Still balanced. The tree is 30 with children 20 and 40. Not every deletion needs a rotation — check first, rotate only when a BF actually reaches ±2.

The height bound — the minimum-nodes recurrence

Minimum number of nodes in an AVL tree of height h N(h) = N(h − 1) + N(h − 2) + 1,    with N(0) = 1 and N(1) = 2

Why: to make the tree of height h as sparse as possible, the root has one subtree of height h−1 and the other of height h−2 — the largest difference the AVL property allows — plus the root itself.

Notice the pattern: N(h) = Fibonacci(h + 3) − 1. The sparsest AVL trees are called Fibonacci trees — a name worth dropping.

Numerical 1. What is the minimum number of nodes in an AVL tree of height 5?

N(0) = 1, N(1) = 2.
N(2) = 2 + 1 + 1 = 4
N(3) = 4 + 2 + 1 = 7
N(4) = 7 + 4 + 1 = 12
N(5) = 12 + 7 + 1 = 20

Answer: 20 nodes.

Numerical 2. What is the maximum possible height of an AVL tree with 20 nodes?

Read the same table the other way. We need the largest h with N(h) ≤ 20.

N(5) = 20 ≤ 20 ✓    N(6) = 33 > 20 ✗

Answer: maximum height 5.

Compare: a perfectly balanced tree with 20 nodes has height ⌊log220⌋ = 4. So the AVL property costs at most one extra level here — and a plain BST could have reached height 19.

The closed-form bound — quote it h < 1.44 log2(n + 2) − 0.328   ⇒   h = O(log n)

So an AVL tree is at worst about 44 % taller than a perfectly balanced tree — and all operations are O(log n) in the worst case.

The trade-off to state when comparing with red–black trees (Lesson 9)

AVL trees are more rigidly balanced, so they are faster to search but slower to insert and delete, because they rotate more often. Use AVL when lookups dominate; use red–black when updates dominate.

Check yourself

1. The balance factor of a node in an AVL tree is:

A balance factor of ±2 anywhere means the tree must be rotated at the lowest such node.

2. BF(A) = +2 and BF(A's left child) = −1 calls for:

The signs disagree, so the direction changes along the path — which always means a double rotation. Matching signs, +2 with +1, would be LL and a single right rotation.

3. Inserting 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 25 into an AVL tree gives a final root of:

Two RR rotations bring the tree to root 20, and inserting 25 triggers an RL double rotation that lifts 30 to the root. Final inorder: 10, 20, 25, 30, 40, 50.

4. The minimum number of nodes in an AVL tree of height 5 is:

N(h) = N(h−1) + N(h−2) + 1 with N(0) = 1 and N(1) = 2 gives 1, 2, 4, 7, 12, 20. Note that 12 is N(4) and 33 is N(6).

5. An AVL insertion requires at most how many rotations?

A single rotation restores the subtree's original height, so nothing above it can still be unbalanced. Deletion reduces the height, so its imbalance can propagate — up to O(log n) rotations.

6. The cases L0 and R0 arise:

An insertion can never leave the child of the unbalanced node with balance factor 0. That is precisely why the R0/R1/R−1 notation exists alongside LL/RR/LR/RL.

7. Compared with a red–black tree, an AVL tree is:

AVL keeps the height within about 1.44 log n; red–black allows up to 2 log(n+1) but rotates far less. Use AVL when lookups dominate.

Recall without looking

Define an AVL tree and the balance factor.

A self-balancing BST in which every node has balance factor height(left) − height(right) in {−1, 0, +1}. A factor of ±2 means the tree must be rotated. It guarantees h = O(log n), so search, insert and delete are O(log n) even in the worst case.

Give the rule for choosing the rotation, and the four cases.

Find the lowest node A with balance factor ±2, then read the first two steps of the path from A to the newly inserted node. LL: +2 with left child +1, single right rotation. RR: −2 with right child −1, single left rotation. LR: +2 with left child −1, left then right. RL: −2 with right child +1, right then left. Same direction twice means single; a change of direction means double.

Give the shortcut for the result of any rotation.

Of the three keys involved, the middle one becomes the subtree root, the smallest becomes its left child and the largest its right child.

How does AVL deletion differ from AVL insertion?

Two ways. Insertion needs at most one rotation because a rotation restores the subtree's original height; deletion reduces the height, so imbalance can propagate to the root and up to O(log n) rotations may be needed. And the rotation is chosen from the sibling's balance factor rather than the path to the deleted node, which is what the L0/L1/L−1 and R0/R1/R−1 notation records. L0 and R0 can only occur on deletion.

State the minimum-nodes recurrence and its first values.

N(h) = N(h−1) + N(h−2) + 1 with N(0) = 1, N(1) = 2, giving 1, 2, 4, 7, 12, 20, 33, 54, 88, 143, 232. It equals Fibonacci(h + 3) − 1, and the sparsest AVL trees are called Fibonacci trees.

Find the maximum height of an AVL tree with 20 nodes, and state the closed-form bound.

The largest h with N(h) ≤ 20 is 5, since N(5) = 20 and N(6) = 33. In general h < 1.44 log₂(n + 2) − 0.328, so an AVL tree is at most about 44 % taller than a perfectly balanced one.
