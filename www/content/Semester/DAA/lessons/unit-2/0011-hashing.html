Hashing and Collision Resolution

The four hash functions, chaining and the three open-addressing schemes with the same key set worked through all of them, the load factor, the probe-count formulas, clustering, and rehashing.

Exam strategy

The guaranteed question is “Insert the following keys into a hash table of size m using linear probing / quadratic probing / double hashing” — often two of the three on the same key set, so you can compare. Draw the table as numbered slots and fill them in order, showing every probe you rejected. Then: “Differentiate open hashing and closed hashing”, and “Define load factor and primary clustering”. Learn the probe-count formulas — a numerical on them appears surprisingly often and almost nobody prepares it.

The idea, and the vocabulary

Definitions A hash table is an array T[0 … m−1] of m slots. A hash function h maps a key k to a slot: h(k) ∈ {0, 1, …, m−1}. The key k is said to hash to slot h(k).

A collision occurs when two distinct keys hash to the same slot: h(k1) = h(k2) with k1 ≠ k2.

The load factor is α = n / m, where n is the number of keys stored and m the number of slots.
Why collisions are unavoidable — the pigeonhole answer

The universe of possible keys is far larger than the table. If |U| > m, then by the pigeonhole principle at least two keys must share a slot. So a hash table is not defined by avoiding collisions — it is defined by resolving them cheaply. Every hashing question ultimately turns on that sentence.

Hash functions

What makes a hash function good — three requirements 1. Uniformity — it should distribute keys evenly across the slots (the simple uniform hashing assumption).
2. Speed — it must be computable in O(1).
3. Determinism — the same key must always give the same slot.

It should also use every part of the key, so that patterns in the data do not become patterns in the table.
MethodFormulaNotes
1. Divisionh(k) = k mod mFastest and most common. Choose m to be a PRIME not close to a power of 2. If m = 2p, the hash is just the low p bits and ignores the rest of the key
2. Multiplicationh(k) = ⌊m · (kA mod 1)⌋Multiply by a constant 0 < A < 1, take the fractional part, scale by m. The value of m does not matter, so a power of 2 is fine. Knuth's suggestion: A = (√5 − 1)/2 ≈ 0.6180339887
3. Mid-squareSquare k, take the middle digitsThe middle digits depend on all digits of k, so it mixes well
4. FoldingSplit k into equal-sized parts and add them (or XOR them)Good for long keys such as phone numbers or identifiers
5. Universal hashingChoose h at random from a family H at run timeGuarantees good expected behaviour whatever the input, defeating an adversary who knows your hash function
Numerical — the multiplication method. Hash k = 123456 with m = 10000 and A = (√5 − 1)/2.

Step 1. kA = 123456 × 0.6180339887 = 76300.0041...

Step 2. Take the fractional part: kA mod 1 = 0.0041...

Step 3. Multiply by m and take the floor: ⌊10000 × 0.0041151⌋ = ⌊41.151⌋ = 41

h(123456) = 41.

Why this A: (√5 − 1)/2 is the reciprocal of the golden ratio, and it scatters consecutive keys about as evenly as any constant can.

The naming trap: open hashing = separate chaining; closed hashing = open addressing. The words “open” and “closed” refer to whether the table is open to storing keys outside it. Objective questions exploit this constantly.

Two keys with the same h1 now follow different probe sequences, because h2 differs — so even secondary clustering is eliminated. Double hashing is the best of the three.

The condition to state: h2(k) must never be 0, and must be relatively prime to m, or the probe sequence will not reach every slot. The usual construction is to take m prime and h2(k) = 1 + (k mod (m − 1)), or to take m a power of 2 and force h2 to be odd. In the example above m = 10 is not prime, so a value such as h2 = 5 would probe only two slots — it happened not to arise here, but the caveat is worth stating.

Worked check at α = 0.9, linear probing:

Successful = ½[1 + 1/(1 − 0.9)] = ½[1 + 10] = 5.5 probes

Unsuccessful = ½[1 + 1/(0.1)²] = ½[1 + 100] = 50.5 probes

The lesson to write down: chaining degrades linearly and stays cheap even past α = 1; open addressing degrades catastrophically as α → 1. Keep α below about 0.75 with open addressing, and rehash when it is exceeded.

Check yourself

1. “Open hashing” is another name for:

Open hashing = chaining, because the table is open to storing keys outside it. Closed hashing = open addressing. Objective questions exploit this constantly.

2. In the division method h(k) = k mod m, m should be:

If m = 2^p, the hash is just the low p bits and ignores the rest of the key. The multiplication method is the one where m's value does not matter.

3. Primary clustering is a problem specific to:

Contiguous runs form and grow faster the longer they are. Quadratic probing removes primary but not secondary clustering; only double hashing removes both.

4. Inserting 12, 22, 32, 45, 67, 89, 55 with linear probing into a table of size 10 places 55 at:

h(55) = 5, which holds 45, so it probes 6 and finds it free. With quadratic probing 55 lands in slot 4 instead, and with double hashing in slot 6 by a different route.

5. With linear probing at α = 0.9, the expected probes for an unsuccessful search is about:

½[1 + 1/(0.1)²] = ½[1 + 100] = 50.5. A successful search costs only 5.5 — the asymmetry is why the table must never be allowed to fill.

6. Deleting a key from an open-addressed table requires:

Emptying it would break every probe sequence that passed through, making later keys unfindable. A search treats a tombstone as occupied; an insertion may reuse it.

7. For double hashing to probe every slot, h₂(k) must:

The usual constructions are m prime with h₂(k) = 1 + (k mod (m−1)), or m a power of 2 with h₂ forced odd. Double hashing gives m² distinct probe sequences against m for the other two.

Recall without looking

Define hash table, collision and load factor, and say why collisions are unavoidable.

A hash table is an array of m slots with a hash function mapping keys into 0…m−1. A collision is two distinct keys hashing to the same slot. The load factor is α = n/m. Collisions are unavoidable because the universe of keys exceeds the table size, so by the pigeonhole principle two keys must share a slot — hashing is about resolving collisions cheaply, not avoiding them.

Name the hash-function methods and the constraint on each.

Division h(k) = k mod m, with m a prime not close to a power of two. Multiplication h(k) = ⌊m(kA mod 1)⌋, where m does not matter and Knuth suggests A = (√5 − 1)/2. Mid-square: square the key and take the middle digits. Folding: split the key into parts and add them. Universal hashing: pick h at random from a family, guaranteeing good expected behaviour against any input.

Compare separate chaining with open addressing on five points.

Chaining is open hashing with keys in linked lists outside the table; α may exceed 1; it needs pointer memory; deletion is easy; cache behaviour is poor. Open addressing is closed hashing with keys inside the table; α ≤ 1; no extra memory; deletion needs tombstones; cache behaviour is good.

Give the three probe functions and what each suffers from.

Linear (h(k)+i) mod m suffers primary and secondary clustering, but always finds a free slot and has the best cache behaviour. Quadratic (h(k)+c₁i+c₂i²) mod m removes primary but keeps secondary clustering, and is only guaranteed to find a slot when m is prime and α < 0.5. Double hashing (h₁(k)+i·h₂(k)) mod m removes both, gives m² distinct probe sequences, and needs h₂ non-zero and coprime to m.

Define primary and secondary clustering.

Primary clustering is the formation of long contiguous runs of occupied slots, which grow faster the longer they are because more hash values land in them — caused by linear probing. Secondary clustering is keys with the same initial hash following the same probe sequence — caused by quadratic probing, and cured only by double hashing.

Give the probe-count formulas and the practical conclusion.

Chaining: 1 + α unsuccessful, 1 + α/2 successful. Linear probing: ½[1 + 1/(1−α)²] unsuccessful, ½[1 + 1/(1−α)] successful. Quadratic and double hashing: 1/(1−α) unsuccessful. Chaining degrades linearly; open addressing degrades catastrophically as α approaches 1 — at α = 0.9 a failed linear-probing search costs about 50 probes. Keep α below 0.75 and rehash.

Explain rehashing and deletion in open addressing.

When α passes a threshold, usually 0.75, allocate a table about twice as large — the next prime above 2m — and reinsert every key with the new hash function. It costs O(n) but is amortised O(1) per insertion. For deletion, mark the slot DELETED rather than EMPTY, since emptying it would break every probe sequence passing through; too many tombstones eventually force a rehash.
